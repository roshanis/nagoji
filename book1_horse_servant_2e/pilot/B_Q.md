# Pilot B, version Q

Under the law of the mothers that ruled her house, Savitri was the senior woman of Kottarakkara. With the Raja fled and no male heir of age, the succession passed to her. She demanded the king's recognition, and she demanded Kottarakkara itself, which he had already decided to keep.

“If I give her the throne of Kottarakkara,” he said in the war hall, “every house I have taken will read it as leave to demand the same. The Kollamkara remnants. The Kayamkulam kin. The Thekkumkur nephews. The dozen petty rajas whose lands now feed our army. They will point to Savitri and say, see, the old ways still hold.”

“And if you refuse?” I asked.

“Then I make an enemy of a woman with a just grievance and powerful friends,” he said. “The Dutch will use her. And Velinadu...”

He did not finish. Velinadu was my wife's house, and my wife was Savitri's blood kin.

“We knew this would come when we first moved against Kottarakkara,” Ramayyan said. “One day a woman of that house would demand what her grandmothers held.”

“Did you know it would be her?” the king asked.

“I knew it would be someone like her,” Ramayyan said. “Sharp, with a good claim and the kin to press it. That is the worst kind of enemy, one who is not wrong.”

---

I met Savitri at Kottarakkara, in the old palace where she had barricaded herself when our lines drew tight around the town.

She was older than I had expected, with lines of worry cut deep around her eyes and mouth. But she held herself like a woman who had been told all her life what she could not do, and had done it anyway.

“The Maratha rider,” she said when she saw me. “Marthanda Varma's foreign sword.”

“I am not here to fight you,” I said.

“No,” she said. “You are here to measure me. To see if I am the kind of threat that can be bought, or frightened, or waited out.”

“And which are you?”

“I am the kind that remembers,” she said. “My kinsman died in a room he could not leave, asking for air and for home. Your king's statecraft is murder dressed in manners.”

“He would have broken Travancore back into pieces,” I said. “He invited our enemies into his court.”

“He defended his house,” she said. “As his mothers did before him. As I will.”

She came a step closer. “Do you know why your king fears the law of the mothers? Because it gives women like me standing, a claim that does not hang on any man's favour. He wants every order to come down from one throne. In our houses everything passes sideways, through sisters and daughters, and it is the women who remember.”

“That world is ending,” I said.

“Perhaps,” she said. “Or perhaps it will outlive him.”

She turned away.

“Tell him I will not go quietly. If he denies my claim, I will find allies who respect it. The Dutch have already made offers. And when Mysore moves south, it will remember which houses stood against Travancore, and reward them.”

“You would sell this coast to the very foreigners you claim to oppose?” I asked.

“I would survive,” she said. “Women like me always have. We tend our gardens long after the forts have fallen.”

---

The battle came at Kottarakkara. Savitri had fled to the Dutch when the palace walls could hold no longer. They armed her cause and gathered men from the chiefs who still resented Marthanda Varma's rising hand. It was their last throw: through her they meant to win back what they had lost on Colachel's beach.

The fighting was ugly. There was no open ground for a charge and no clean line for a volley. This was house land and temple land, where every tree had an owner who could tell you whose grandmother had planted it. Men fought for pay or for a king, and to show that the old names still counted for something.

I saw Savitri once, across a field of trampled paddy. She sat her horse in white mourning clothes, now grey with dust and smoke. A sword hung at her side. Behind her, men with Dutch muskets and spears of their own waited for orders.

She saw me. She had the look of a woman who had weighed her choices and found them all bitter.

We won because we had more guns and more discipline. Who was in the right never entered the sum.

By evening her force had scattered. The Dutch factors fled toward their ships, and the chiefs' men slipped back to their villages, hoping to be forgotten.

Savitri herself was captured trying to reach the coast.

---

They brought her to the king's tent as the sun went down over the paddy. Her hands were bound and her face was bruised, but she stood straight and looked Marthanda Varma in the eye.

“You could have run,” he said.

“This is my land,” she said. “I will not watch it from a ship.”

He rose and came to her.

“I met you once,” he said. “When we were young, before any of this. You had a laugh that carried across courtyards.”

She looked down at her bound hands. “I have not laughed in a long time,” she said.

“Nor have I,” he said.

“I will not kill you,” he went on. “I will not imprison you. You will go to Kochi, to the Dutch who made you such fine promises, and you will live there. But you will not return. If you set foot in Travancore again, I will treat you as I would any invader.”

“Exile,” she said.

“You will live,” he said. “That is more than your kinsman got.”

“You are building something,” she said. “I have seen your forts and your armies, one kingdom where there were a hundred. Perhaps it will hold against Mysore and the Dutch, and men will speak your name with respect when you and I are ash.”

She leaned toward him.

“But remember this. Every house you broke, every succession you denied, every woman you sent into exile will become a story, and stories outlast walls. When your laws are dust, someone will remember what you took. They will remember me.”

“Let them,” he said. “I build for the living.”

“That,” she said, “is why you will never understand us.”

They led her away toward the coast and the ship that would take her to Kochi. I watched until she was gone from sight. Smoke was still rising from Kottarakkara's fields.
