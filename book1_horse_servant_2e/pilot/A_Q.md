# Pilot A, version Q

The first thing they took was my name.

A clerk took it with a shrug and an entry in his book. I had been Nagoji Sawant, *huzurat* cavalry, one of the household horse that rode closest to Chimaji Appa and the Peshwa, where the sardars had put me after more than one fight showed them I was brave and impatient. I had ridden the black horse Kanka, and I came from a village near Nashik where the farthest water anyone feared was a swollen river in the rains. To the men who ruled this cellar I was a number the clerk scratched beside the words *prisioneiro marata*.

They chained us in rows along the curved wall, deep under the Portuguese fortress, backs to the damp stone and ankles linked by rusty iron. Above the sweating ceiling the bells of Goa's churches rang for evening prayers. Down here we kept time by the scrape of the gaoler's boots and the splash of the bucket that carried away blood and filth. Water dripped in the dark, steady as the eaves of a Deccan house after rain.

I was twenty-five that year, 1738, in the seasons after we broke a string of Portuguese forts along the Konkan. I had seen men I admired die foolishly, and I still believed I would get to choose how I died.

They had already taken my fingernails by the time Father Duarte came.

The padre picked his way between the lines of prisoners with his black cassock held above the slime on the floor. He was a thin man with a scholar's stoop and ink-stained fingers, built for argument by the look of him. Yet the palm of his right hand was callused, and no pen leaves calluses like those. His eyes went from one ruined body to the next.

I watched him without seeming to. As a boy I had learned to sit in the corner of a fort courtyard and see everything, from the loosened strap on a trooper's saddle to the way a sardar's hand tightened on his sword hilt at the mention of a rival.

The gaoler, João, came with him. He was a broad man whose belly strained his leather belt, and he smelled of garlic sausage and chapel incense. When he passed my place he tapped my shoulder with his boot, almost companionable.

“Este,” he said in Portuguese, “this one is stubborn.”

I kept my face blank. Stubborn is better than broken in any language.

Father Duarte looked where the boot had touched. His eyes were grey and tired, with the faint redness of a man who slept badly. When he saw my hands in their dirty wrappings he winced, and his fingers twitched inside his sleeves.

“How long since the last questioning?” he asked in the same tongue.

“Two days,” João said. “He still says he is only a horseman. He gives us no names, no forts, no routes. He knows we have others who talk, and still he holds on.”

The priest turned back to me. “You understand our language, senhor marata?”

“Enough to know when I am being lied to, Father,” I said in my own Marathi. My mouth tasted of rust and old water. “But ask your questions. I have heard most of them already.”

João laughed. “You see? He understands.”

“Bring him,” Father Duarte said.

They unshackled my legs and hauled me upright, and my knees, folded under me too long, would not straighten. In the field I had kept the horseman's rule without a thought: a Maratha may bleed and fall and curse the gods, but he does not whimper in front of an enemy. This time the groan was halfway out before I shut my teeth on it.

The interrogation chamber lay twenty paces away. The smell changed as we went, from rotting straw and human waste to oil, the sweat of frightened men and sour wine sharp enough to sting the nose. The room was vaulted, its stone thick enough to swallow most screams before they reached the street.

Ropes hung from an iron ring bolted into the ceiling. A table to one side was laid out neatly with pliers, hammers and wooden wedges, tools that had never worked metal. A brazier glowed on the far side, the air wavering over its coals.

They sat me on a stool with my wrists bound to its legs. João checked the knots and went to the table. The priest stayed on his feet with his hands tucked into his sleeves.

“Nagoji Sawant,” Father Duarte said, using my name as if it still belonged to me. “You have told us that you rode with a Maratha force against our allies. You have admitted attacking Portuguese caravans and outposts. The Viceroy's spies swear you rode with the huzurat, under Chimaji Appa himself, and that your sardars chose you for your boldness. Such men see more than dust and hooves. What we do not yet know is who ordered those attacks, how many men you had, and which forts or roads you meant to strike next.”

“Then you know more than I do,” I said. “We raided where we could, when we could, against whoever traded with our enemies. That is the way of the ghats and passes. You have maps and books. My horse does not read.”

João picked up a pair of iron tongs. “Your horse is dead,” he said conversationally. “We shot it when we brought you in. A pity. Fine animal.”

My throat closed. Kanka's black mane against my cheek on winter mornings, his easy stride, the way he had carried me through musket fire near Chaul. I forced the memory down. João was watching my face over the tongs.

“Then he died better than I will,” I said.

“You are not a fool,” Father Duarte said. “You know where you stand. The Viceroy needs names. He needs to know whether the army that took his northern forts is turning south, and whether Chimaji Appa means to bring his siege guns to Goa. Answer, and your suffering ends. I can speak for you, perhaps find you work on a plantation, a life in chains that is still a life. If you do not...”

He glanced at the brazier.

So the Viceroy was afraid for Goa itself. I had smelled it on the priest. I thought of the northern forts falling one after another through those hard years, the news of each running down the Konkan ahead of our horses. Chimaji Appa was coming for Goa, and any day now, I told myself, the ground above this cellar would shake under Maratha cannon.

“If I do not,” I said, “you will do what you have done to others. You will pull me apart on your ropes and put hot iron to my skin, and try to make me scream the names you have already written down.”

João snorted. “He talks too much.”

“He is a soldier,” Father Duarte said. “He knows how this is done.” He stepped closer. “Tell me this at least, Nagoji. Not for the Viceroy, for me. Do you believe you fight for God and some greater good, or only for plunder and the honour of your people?”
