# Pilot B, version K

She claimed Kottarakkara by right and demanded that the king recognise her. Under the law of the mothers that ruled her house she was its senior woman, and with the raja fled and no male heir of age, the succession passed to her.

Marthanda Varma had already decided to keep Kottarakkara. “If I give her the throne,” he said in the war hall, “every house I have taken will demand the same: what is left of Kollamkara, the Kayamkulam kin, the Thekkumkur nephews, the dozen petty rajas whose lands now feed our army. They will point to Savitri and say, see, the old ways still hold.”

“And if you refuse?” I asked.

“Then I make an enemy of a woman with a real grievance and powerful friends,” he said. “The Dutch will use her. And Velinadu...”

He left it unfinished and kept his eyes off me. Velinadu meant Revathi, and Revathi was Savitri's blood kin.

“We knew this when we first moved against Kottarakkara,” Ramayyan said. “Sooner or later a woman of that house would demand what her grandmothers held.”

“Did you know it would be her?” the king asked.

“I knew it would be someone like her,” Ramayyan said. “Clever, with a good claim and kin in the right houses. An enemy who is in the right costs more than any other.”

---

I met Savitri in the old palace at Kottarakkara, where she had barricaded herself when our siege lines closed.

She was older than I had expected, lined deep about the eyes and mouth, and she held herself like a woman who had been told all her life what she might not do and had done it anyway.

“The Maratha rider,” she said. “Marthanda Varma's foreign sword.”

“I am not here to fight you,” I said.

“No,” she said. “You are here to measure me, to see if I am the kind that can be bought, or frightened, or waited out.”

“And which are you?”

“I am the kind that remembers. My kinsman died in a room he could not leave, asking for air and to go home. Your king calls that statecraft. It was murder.”

“He was pulling Travancore apart,” I said. “He sat our enemies at his own table.”

“He defended his house,” she said, “as his mothers did before him, and as I will. Do you know why your king fears the law of the mothers? Because under it a woman like me holds a claim that hangs on no man's favour. He wants everything to come down from one throne. With us it runs sideways, through sisters and daughters, and the women keep the memory of it.”

“That world is ending,” I said.

“Perhaps,” she said. “Or perhaps it will see him out.”

She turned away. “Tell him I will not go quietly. If he denies my claim, I will find allies who honour it. The Dutch have made offers already. And tell him that when Mysore moves south at last, they will remember which houses stood against Travancore, and reward them.”

“You would sell your coast to the powers you say you oppose?” I asked.

“I would survive,” she said. “Women like me always have. We outlive the men who think they rule us, and we are still tending our gardens when their forts have fallen.”

---

It came to a battle at Kottarakkara. When the palace walls could hold no longer, Savitri had fled to the Dutch. They armed her and raised men for her among the chiefs who still resented how far Marthanda Varma's hand now reached. It was their last throw: to win back through her what they had lost on the beach at Colachel.

The fighting was ugly. There was no open ground for a charge and no clean line for a volley. This was house land and temple land, where a man knew whose grandmother had planted each tree. Men fought there for pay or for a king, and for the old houses too, so that the names would still mean something.

I saw Savitri once, across a field of trampled paddy. She sat her horse in white mourning clothes gone grey with dust and smoke, a sword at her side. Behind her, men with Dutch muskets and their own spears waited for orders. She turned her head and found me. She had weighed every choice left to her, by the look of her, and found them all bitter.

We won. Whoever had the right of it, we had more muskets and harder-drilled men, and that is the arithmetic that settles a field. By evening her force had scattered. The Dutch factors fled toward their ships, and the local men slipped back to their villages, hoping to be forgotten.

Savitri was caught trying to reach the coast.

---

They brought her to the king's tent at sunset. Her hands were bound and her face bruised, but she stood straight and did not lower her eyes before Marthanda Varma.

“You could have run,” he said.

“This is my land,” she said. “I will not watch it from a ship.”

He rose and went to her.

“I met you once, when we were young, before any of this,” he said. “You had a laugh that carried across courtyards.”

“I have not laughed in a long time,” she said.

“Nor have I,” he said. “I will not kill you, and I will not imprison you. You will go to Kochi, to the Dutch who promised you so much. You have seen what their promises buy. You will live there, and you will not come back. If you set foot in Travancore again, I will treat you as I would any invader.”

“Exile,” she said.

“It is your life,” he said. “More than your kinsman was given.”

“You are building something,” she said. “I have seen your forts and your armies, one kingdom where a hundred stood. Perhaps it will hold against Mysore and outlast the Dutch. Perhaps men will speak your name with respect a hundred years from now. But remember this. Every house you broke, every woman you sent into exile, will be a story one day, and stories outlast walls. Someone will remember what you took. They will remember me.”

“Let them,” he said. “I build for the living.”

“That,” she said, “is why you will never understand us.”

They led her away toward the coast, to a ship that would carry her north along a shore she could see from the deck and would never touch again. Smoke from Kottarakkara's fields was drifting across the evening sky. I watched the road until she was gone from it.
