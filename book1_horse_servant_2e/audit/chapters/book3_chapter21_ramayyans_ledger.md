## Verdict

| Measure | Call |
|:-|:-|
| Severity | **4 of 5** |
| Recommended intensity | **Deep** in three blocks (opening L3 to 15, the ledger L43 to 78, the coda L195 to 215). Everywhere else it is cut-first tightening of speech tails, not re-voicing. |
| Estimated word cut | **About 22%** (2,140 to about 1,670 words, after the few short additions proposed below) |

The scene underneath is good. A soldier ducks out of a squall into a room he did not know existed and finds his own name filed on a shelf. The minister shows him just enough to frighten him, and the chapter plants three things that pay off later: the Elayadathu leaf "underlined twice" (Ch 27), the letter from the north (Ch 25's test) and Padmini's heir (Ch 27). Several exchanges are as good as anything in the book: "He worries about his soul ... That is useful to know", "Especially the king ... He would be angry if I did not", "You will burn that if someone else ever comes in here", "half your weight in the north and half here", "Which are you?", and the festival memory at L193.

The surface around them has four machine habits:

1. **A ladder of one-line paragraphs** that announces the room three times before showing it (L3 to 15).
2. **A modern dossier.** Ramayyan's leaves read like an HR file and an intelligence brief ("Skills", "adaptability", "mitigated", "emotional ties", "Leverage: significant", "Risk assessment: HIGH. Timeline"). REGISTER_AND_ANACHRONISM calls this the single most visible modern intrusion in the book.
3. **Ramayyan's speeches end on an aphorism** eight times (L88, 92, 129, 141, 145, 157, 183, 189), and Nagoji answers in the same polished register. The watching debate runs four rounds and dilutes the real threat at L153 to 157.
4. **The coda** (L195 to 215) is one of the most machine-sounding pages in the book: personified shelves, an "Of X. Of Y." list, a There/Here antithesis, "It was a small difference. It was also vast.", imagined historians, a Mine/His mirror and "the next storm".

Most fixes are cuts. The ledger has to be rewritten, not trimmed, because its form is right (terse leaf entries, the model for Ch 25 L208 to 210) and only its vocabulary is wrong. The 22% cut is above the PLAN's 5 to 10%. About 145 words of it is the coda, and most of the rest is explanatory tails after lines that already landed. No scene, plot plant or historical fact is lost.

## Opening

Current first lines (L3 to 15):

> I had always known that Ramayyan wrote things down.
> You could see it in the ink stains on his fingers, in the callus where his stylus rested, in the way his eyes drifted for a moment when someone spoke, as if he were already filing the words away on some inner page.
> What I had not seen, until that afternoon, was the scale of his book.
> It was not a single volume, of course. It was a room.
> I found it by accident.
> We were in the fort ... grey and angry. ... expecting a small guard room.
> Instead, I stepped into a space where history sat in rows.

**Verdict: revise.** Keep the first sentence and the squall. Cut the ladder. Five one-line paragraphs promise the discovery three times and give it away ("It was a room") before the squall stages it. The details in L5 are also the wrong craft (ink on a palm-leaf man). The whole chapter is missing any Deccan reference point, and the natural one is the village kulkarni's records, which a Maratha boy would have seen before he ever saw a Portuguese clerk's book.

**Proposed** (about 120 words, replacing L3 to 15):

> I had always known that Ramayyan wrote things down. The thumbnail of his left hand was grown long and notched to steady the stylus, and the soot they rub into the leaves had worked into the creases of his fingers. In council, while a man talked, Ramayyan's eyes would go past him, the way our village kulkarni's did when my father argued over the land tax.
>
> I did not know where the leaves went until the afternoon a squall caught us in the fort. We had been arguing over where to put a new storehouse when it came in off the sea. Men scrambled to cover powder barrels. The meeting scattered. Looking for shelter, I ducked through a doorway I had not used before, expecting a small guard room.

Continue with L17, "Palm leaf bundles filled shelves from floor to ceiling." The cut to the shelves does the reveal. Verify the notched thumbnail (a scribe's practice described for South Indian palm-leaf writing). If it cannot be confirmed, use "a hard ridge on his middle finger where the iron stylus rode". "Kulkarni" needs a glossary entry, not a gloss in the text (VOICE_BIBLE 3.7).

## Ending

Current last lines (L195 to 215):

> As I left the room, the shelves watched me with their mute rows.
> Outside, the air smelled of wet stone and ink.
> That night, lying under the jackfruit tree after the ground had dried, I thought of Ramayyan's books.
> Of my line in it. Of Revathi's. Of Padmini's. Of Lannoy's. Of the king's own.
> In Goa, a different kind of book had been kept ... There, I had been a prisoner number. Here, I was a sentence in a longer story.
> It was a small difference. It was also vast.
> I did not know whether, in years to come, historians would praise Ramayyan as wise or condemn him as a spider ...
> We all kept our own accounts, in our own ways.
> Mine were carved in memories of hooves on sand and the weight of men's trust.
> His were written in palm leaves and tags.
> Both, I suspected, would matter when the next storm came.

**Verdict: rewrite.** Both auditors and OPENINGS_ENDINGS_MOTIFS agree. Seven closers are stacked on top of each other, the last is the storm motif that STYLE_SHEET reserves for Ch 13, and the Mine/His coda settles tensions the chapter should leave open (the Velinadu line, "before the monsoon", the letter from the north). The flashback has already made the coda's point without saying it: Ramayyan credits the leaves, and Nagoji remembers catching a wrist. The ending only has to show Ramayyan writing again.

**Proposed** (about 60 words, replacing L195 to 215; ends on an action; keeps the one earned Goa contrast, answering Ch 1 L7's "a number the clerk scratched"; sets up Ch 25 L208 to 210, where Ramayyan writes a line on Nagoji's leaf and this time "did not hide it from me"):

> The rain had stopped by the time I went to the door. I looked back once. He had taken my bundle down again and was cutting a new line into the leaf. In Goa a clerk had scratched me into his book as a number and never lifted his head. Ramayyan lifted his, once, and went on writing.

**Alternative** (if the author wants to keep the jackfruit tree and end on the Kottarakkara thread):

> That night, lying under the jackfruit tree after the ground had dried, I tried to call back the rest of the Elayadathu leaf. All I could see were the words he had underlined twice.

I prefer the first. It keeps the reader in the room with the man who holds the pen, and it does not ask Nagoji to think the chapter's meaning out loud.

## Chapter-specific notes (continuity and history)

1. **Can Nagoji read the leaves? (missed by both auditors.)** He learns Malayalam "slowly" (Ch 9 L80), and as late as Ch 22 L87 he catches only fragments of spoken Malayalam. Yet here he reads three full entries at a glance, one of them in "a glimpse" (L65). There are two fixes. (a) Minimal: one clause at L37, for example "I read their letters slowly, the way a boy reads, but I could read my own name." (b) Stronger: Ramayyan reads Nagoji's entry aloud to him in Konkani (the bridge language, Ch 4 L29), which makes the reading a deliberate act, and the Elayadathu glimpse becomes what a slow reader catches: "Elayadathu. Kottarakkara. Savitri. The Dutch. Three letters. Revathi Bayi. Two words at the bottom were underlined twice, and those took me longer: before the monsoon." Option (b) removes the dossier format from that block entirely and gives the chapter the partial comprehension that VOICE_BIBLE 3.7 calls the most honest thing in the book. It is the author's call. The confirmed ledger rewrites above work with either option.
2. **Festival flashback (L193).** N-09: the broom and the Padmanabhapuram sweeping rite pre-empt Ch 28 L75. The fix above makes it a lamp festival (verify the name if one is added; Thrikkarthika is the Kerala lamp festival). Separately, Ch 10 L151 dates "a dagger in a festival crowd" to the king's early years, before Nagoji arrived, while L193 puts Nagoji in that crowd. OPENINGS_ENDINGS_MOTIFS already recommends keeping the attempts list only here (cutting Ch 10 L151 and Ch 22 L335). Do that, and the dating conflict goes with it.
3. **Smiles and chuckles (L104, L171, L187).** Ch 25 L198: "for the first time in all the years I had known him, he smiled." All three are cut above. This is the chapter's share of the book-wide thin-smile problem (OPENINGS_ENDINGS_MOTIFS, Ramayyan row).
4. **The new ledger wording affects two other chapters.** Ch 18 L121 ("The entry marked HIGH") must change; N-07 already proposes a fix. Ch 25 L90 ("move me from the 'risk' column in your books to the 'asset' one") quotes a ledger that will no longer say "Risks". Suggest: "You want to move me off the leaf that says 'To watch', one way or the other." Ch 25 L208 to 210 ("tested with northern letter ... Is ours.") is the model the rewrite follows, so the two leaves now sound like one hand.
5. **Kottarakkara (L68, L72).** N-18 (confined, fled or dead) and N-19 (the internal date) are still open. The fixes above survive either decision ("Keeps to his rooms"; "before the monsoon" or "when the Dutch move"). L69's "Savitri" and L71's blood tie agree with Ch 27 L73 and L113.

7. **Writing materials.** Palm leaves are incised with an iron stylus and blackened with soot and oil, which is where the "ink" at L5, L197 and L207 and the "paper" at L145 go wrong. The glossary (L95) has Ramayyan writing Persian for high-level correspondence, so he could have ink on him, but the room is palm leaf. The fixes above remove all four. The clay tags at L17 are a separate check (Borderline).
8. **Padmini's heir.** L60's Nandini, "her sister's girl" in the fix, matches Ch 27 L35. The death retrospect matches Ch 27 L33.
9. **"Because a Brahmin said the stars looked wrong" (L137).** Tradition makes the historical Ramayyan a Brahmin, and so was the Maratha village kulkarni in the proposed opening. Said to Ramayyan's face, "a Brahmin" is either tactless or pointed. The fix uses "his astrologer". If the author wants the edge, keep "Brahmin" and let Ramayyan not react.
