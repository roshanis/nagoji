The chapter's core is strong, and it is the payoff of a question the king asked in Ch 19 (L111 to L119): "if they sent a letter ... what would you do?" / "I would read the letter ... Then I would choose." The letter arrives here. What works is Ramayyan's candour ("Instead, I show you. Ask yourself why."), the exchange "Half truths." / "Selected truths.", the careful letter with its field knowledge (wet ground betraying heavy boots), the hand laid over Ramayyan's, the burning, the ledger's "Is ours.", and Padmini's pots on a shelf. The ghee, the cloth and the patron's seal at L104 are the most period-true interior passage in Part IV.

Machine habits wrap all of that. Ramayyan and Nagoji trade mirrored epigrams: past and present, trick and sincere, mine and your bones, the wall and the doors. There are three memory montages (L130, L236 and L248, L266). Ramayyan uses a spymaster's modern vocabulary: asset, reached out, turned, network, "have your moment", "work with him directly", "That does not erase what he did. But it may change what it means." After the climax, the Duarte revelation spends about 1,150 words, over a third of the chapter, explaining Duarte's motives and their moral meaning. Then the chapter ends on six one-line verdicts. The density numbers agree: 77% of narration paragraphs are a single sentence, and there are 32 standalone one-line narration paragraphs, against 9 in Ch 1 (REPETITION_AND_DENSITY). There are seven soft adverbs (softly three times, slowly four times) against a limit of two.

**Over-cut check.** 24% is well above the plan's 5 to 10%, so I checked where it comes from. About 60% of the cut is in two blocks. The Duarte section goes from about 1,150 words to about 740, losing the fourfold "eleven years", three restatements of the irony, two ch24 recaps and two "one more thing" hooks. The coda goes from 124 words to about 50. The rest is spread thinly: a gloss, a montage, a seesaw, reaction beats. No scene, plot fact or callback is lost. The fixes keep the letter and its bait, the dilemma, the selected-truths letter and everything it leaves out, the burning, the ledger, Duarte's pay, his steering of deserters south, Nagoji's new use to Ramayyan, Duarte's staying on, De Lannoy's chaplain, the plant for Ch 28 L243, Padmini and Revathi. The few additions replace abstractions with the book's own facts: Modi on Deccan paper (Ch 19 L19), the wedges and the thumbnail (Ch 1 L43, L81), Udayagiri (Ch 28 L193), and one beat of Padmini's age (backlog C12-03).

Three decisions sit under the line edit and should be made first. **(1)** Keep the burning. I recommend it, because it is the published text and the ledger and Ch 26's ash build on it. Then L200, L206, L334 and L340 must follow it (N-06). **(2)** Fix Duarte's dates (Chapter notes 5). **(3)** Decide whether the Deccan letter is paper (Chapter notes 2). The fixes below assume burn, a recruitment after Goa, and paper.

## Opening

Current first lines (L3 to L11):

> The ola came on a day that smelled of ink and rain.
>
> I had spent the morning on the marches, watching a new patrol route settle into the feet of my horsemen. By afternoon, clouds rolled in from the west, blurring the line of the hills. By the time I reached the fort, my clothes were damp and my temper short.
>
> Ramayyan waited for me in his small room off the war hall, the one that looked onto a narrow courtyard where a single neem tree grew.

**Verdict: revise L3 to L11.** Both auditors agree. L3 is a stock sensory hook that makes no literal sense, and it gives away the letter fourteen lines before the stone is lifted. OPENINGS_ENDINGS_MOTIFS lists it in the smell-opener family, and Ch 21 L197 already has "the air smelled of wet stone and ink". L5 moves time along with "By afternoon ... By the time ..." and ends on a stock pair. The patrol route "settling into the feet" of mounted men is a misfire. The chapter wakes up at L13 to L19 (the stool, the stone, "He did not touch it.").

**Proposed** (replaces L3 to L11; the rest of the scene is unchanged apart from the fixes above):

> I had spent the morning on the northern marches, riding a new patrol route twice over with my horsemen so they would know it in the dark. In the afternoon the rain came in from the west and rubbed out the line of the hills. By the time I reached the fort I was wet to the skin and my horse was sulking.
>
> Ramayyan was waiting for me in his small room off the war hall, the one that looked onto a narrow courtyard with a neem tree in it.
>
> "You ride in all weathers now," he said, as I shook rain from my turban.
>
> "The chiefs up there do not stop raiding for rain," I said.

This keeps the rain, which the chapter uses to the end, and the marches, and it gives him a horseman's reason to be out and a horse to be amused by (VOICE_BIBLE 3.3, 3.5). A harder cut, opening at L13 ("He nodded toward a low stool."), would also work if the author wants the chapter to start in the room.

## Ending

Current last lines (L336 to L348):

> She was right.
>
> Every choice binds.
>
> Ramayyan's test had not been whether I loved my hills more than this coast. It had been whether, when pressed, I would betray one home to save another.

**Verdict: rewrite (mostly by cutting).** This is the most machine-made passage in the chapter. It is six one-line verdicts in a row. The test is restated in the correction frame and contradicts L120. The ledger entry is announced again. **Proposed** (after L330; L332 to L348 replaced):

> "Good," she said. "I am too old to build new ones for this house."
>
> She put her hand through my arm, and I walked her to the gate at her pace. It was slower than it had been the year before.
>
> Revathi heard of it, as she heard of everything. Her next letter was mostly about levies. At the end she wrote:

The walk to the gate is Padmini's last scene with him before Ch 27 L31 ("By then Padmini Amma was gone"), and it gives the book the one physical beat of her age that backlog C12-03 asks for. "Mostly about levies" is how he describes her letters in Ch 19. The chapter ends on her line of writing, with the chain from her own joke in Ch 19 L169.

**Alternative** if the author prefers the tighter close: end on the second paragraph above (Padmini, the gate, the slower pace), and cut Revathi's letter altogether.

## Chapter-specific notes

1. **Burn or send.** The chapter holds two versions. In the first, Nagoji writes a selected-truths letter that Ramayyan approves and sends (L136 to L162, and Revathi at L334). In the second, he burns it (L168 to L198, grafted in with stray leading spaces). I recommend the burn. It is in the published EPUB, and the ledger ("Burned ... himself"), Ramayyan's "Today, I do not have to" and Ch 26's ash all depend on it. It also pays off the king's question in Ch 19 L111 to L119 with an act. It does not undo the king's Ch 19 wish that Nagoji "speak to men in Pune", because this letter comes from the Bhonsle's camp, a rival to Pune since the author's correction. The burn version needs fixes at L200 ("he added"), L206 ("two short lines"), L334 (N-06) and L340 (cut), all in the table. With the Ch 22 audit cutting "I will send word north of what I have chosen" (Ch 22 L377), this chapter becomes the one renunciation of the Maratha state, as OPENINGS_ENDINGS_MOTIFS section 4 proposes. Ch 19 is the family, Ch 22 the frontier.
2. **The Deccan letter should be paper, in Modi.** Ch 19 L19 makes a point of it: his brother's letter is "a folded piece of paper, not palm leaf". Maratha correspondence of the 1740s was written on paper in the Modi hand. A Kerala ola is cut with a stylus and blackened with soot. Lines to change: L3 (cut), L17 ("A folded paper lay on the table"), L27 ("unfolded the paper"), L31 (name Modi), L98 ("I looked at the paper again"), L106 (cut), L182 ("I took the letter from the Deccan"), L192 (paper and leaf burn differently). Nagoji's reply can stay on a Travancore leaf with a stylus (L122), since it is written in Ramayyan's office. Ramayyan reading Modi is plausible: the Maratha court at Thanjavur kept its records in Modi. If the author wants to go further, Ramayyan could hand him paper and a reed pen instead, but the contrast of the two materials in the flame is worth keeping.
3. **The sardar and the Carnatic (N-02).** Nagoji was twenty-five in 1738, a huzurat rider under Chimaji Appa in the Konkan (Ch 1 L7, L11). Raghuji Bhonsle's Carnatic expedition and the capture of Chanda Sahib came in 1740 and 1741, after he was in chains. "Briefly in the Carnatic campaigns before my troubles began" cannot stand. The L64 fix makes the sardar a Konkan comrade who has since gone south with the Bhonsle. That also answers the holistic reader's point that a Bhonsle officer recruiting a Peshwa's household trooper should strike Nagoji as odd.
4. **Chanda Sahib.** He was taken at Tiruchirappalli in March 1741 and held at Satara, not Nagpur, until he was ransomed in about 1748. If the chapter sits in 1748 (backlog CL-06), "rots in our chains" is at the edge of true. It is safe for 1746 or 1747. The Borderline trim adds "at Satara". The cut gloss at L38 was accurate but addressed to the reader.
5. **Duarte's dates.** L228 says "eleven years", L234 "Since before you washed up on this coast", and L234 also "not long after the Dutch began pressing harder". Ch 24 L309 says "More than a decade" since Goa, and CL-06 puts Ch 24 at about 1748, so eleven years back is 1737. That would make Duarte Ramayyan's paid man while he watched the torture in 1738. It would also contradict "not long after the Dutch began pressing harder", which was about 1739, and it would break Ramayyan's logic of guilt as penance at L270, since the penance needs the torture to come first. Recommended: he was recruited after Goa. The L234 fix reads "Since the year after they put you on the ship", and L228 becomes "nine years". Settle the exact number when CL-06 fixes a year for Chs 22 to 24, and use it once. If the author does want Duarte in Travancore's pay before the cellar, Ch 21 L45 ("First appearance: Goan dungeon, retrieved by Travancore allies") could be the seed, but then L234, L244 and L270 must be rewritten around that darker fact, and Nagoji's reaction must face it.
