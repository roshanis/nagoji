## Verdict

| Measure | Call |
|:-|:-|
| Severity | **4 of 5** |
| Recommended intensity | **Deep** (line-level re-voicing in the Ramayyan and war-hall scenes, and a rebuilt coda) |
| Estimated word cut | **About 25%** (2,021 to about 1,500 words, after the few short additions proposed below) |

The chapter has good material and a weak surface. Its best lines are authored and specific: the fifty-thousand-rupee figure, Nagoji seeing his own people's chauth in Chanda Sahib's tribute, "they do not ride home when the rains come", "elephants cannot climb where goats can", and the king's "Let them want". The surface around them runs on a template. A line of dialogue, then a gesture or prop (stone, thin smile, gesture at the hills), then an aphorism. Nearly every speech packages its point as a triad or a mirrored maxim ("tribute buys time, not safety", "one hand ... the other", "Control Shenkottai, and you control ..."). Every section ends on a quotable button (L39, L63, L75, L131, L159).

The tribute point is made about seven times between L17 and L63. The meaning of the capture is stated five times between L97 and L131. The coda (L135 to L159) is the worst stretch in the chapter and one of the worst in the book. It has a solitary walk, a Some/Others split, a recap, "The irony was not lost on me", a proverb pair, three "I thought of" sentences, "They were wrong.", a title drop and "There was still work to do." Rebuild it; do not line-edit it.

The 25% cut is well above the 5 to 10% the PLAN expects book-wide. That is deliberate. About half of it is the coda (373 words to about 130), and most of the rest is repeated tribute exposition. No scene, plot beat or historical fact is lost.

Three continuity faults travel with the prose fix and should be settled first, because several rewrites depend on them: who fought Chanda Sahib's raids (L43 to 55, backlog N-24), the leftover "fled the Peshwa" backstory (L71, L73, L141, L145, backlog N-02), and the landing timeline (L79 against L153, backlog N-11).

## Opening

Current first lines (L3 to 7): "Before the Dutch became the enemy we watched from our beaches, there was another shadow that fell across Travancore each dry season. It came not from the sea but from the northeast ... / Chanda Sahib. / I had heard the name in Pune, spoken with the wary respect one gives to a competent rival ..."

**Verdict: rewrite.** Both auditors agree, and so do I. It is a documentary prologue: "It came" three times, a "not X but Y" pivot, a nothing/everything antithesis, a one-word drumroll paragraph, then a potted biography ending on a colon triad. The first clause also gets the frame wrong, since in this chapter's present the Dutch are already the enemy.

**Proposed** (opens in scene, as OPENINGS_ENDINGS_MOTIFS recommends; then Nagoji's Pune knowledge; then his own view of the seals):

> "He came first in thirty-six," Ramayyan told me one evening, as we sat on the temple steps at lamp-lighting. He was turning a small stone in his fingers. "Before you washed up on our shore. Before the king had finished breaking the Pillamar. We were weak, and Chanda Sahib knew it."
>
> I had heard the name in Pune, from sardars back from the Carnatic who did not laugh when they said it. He was no Mughal. He had married the Nawab of Arcot's daughter and made himself master of Trichinopoly and Madurai, and the Madurai country ran right up to the Travancore passes. His demands came written in Persian under Mughal seals. In the Deccan we knew what such seals were worth. A sardar read the seal, counted the horse that had carried it, and paid for the horse.

Then continue at L13 with "His cavalry came down through the pass." L9 and L11 are absorbed into the new first paragraph.

## Ending

Current last lines (L151 to 159):

> The sea wind carried the smell of salt and smoke.
> Tomorrow, or the day after, the Dutch would make their move. They would land at Colachel with their blue coats and their cannon, thinking they faced a king still bleeding from two fronts.
> They were wrong.
> The shadow from Arcot had lifted. Now there was only the sea, and the enemy upon it, and the sand where we would meet them.

## Chapter-specific notes (continuity and history)

1. **Who fought Chanda Sahib's raids (L43 to 55; backlog N-24, N-01).** Option A, recommended: a Travancore veteran tells it (the old Nair captain at Aramboli), and L45 is cut. Option B: keep Nagoji in a 1740 skirmish near Nagercoil. This is historically possible, since he was in Travancore by late 1739 and Ch 14 L5 and L11 imply a southern campaign against Chanda Sahib's men. It also gives the chapter the first-hand cavalry judgement it lacks. Under B, the enemy-horse speech (L47) becomes Nagoji's own observation, "How did you survive?" is asked of a Nair, and Dhanaji appears only if N-01 puts him on this coast by 1740. Either way, the lines at L43, L45 and L49 cannot all stand.
2. **Optional voice line at L51 (both auditors noticed the gap).** When the veteran describes using the ghats against cavalry, Nagoji would recognise the Maratha way of war. Something like: "I told him our grandfathers had fought the Mughals the same way in the Sahyadri. He did not seem to take it as a compliment." Add it only if the author wants one more beat in the section.
3. **"Fled the Peshwa" backstory (backlog N-02).** It appears at L71 ("my exile"), L73 ("powers I no longer served"), L141 ("men I no longer served") and L145 ("I had fled the Peshwa's justice ... the power I had escaped"), and also at Ch 11 L129 ("a fugitive with no letter from Pune") and Ch 25 L64. 4. **Raghuji, not the Peshwa (L69, L97, L109, L145).** The 1740 to 1741 Carnatic expedition was led by Raghuji Bhonsle (with Fatehsingh Bhonsle), sent by Chhatrapati Shahu. Raghuji was a rival of the Peshwa's house. Ch 25 L38 gets it right ("the Bhonsle of Nagpur"). The king may err (L97); Nagoji should not. Optional line after L97 that puts the voice on the page: "Raghuji Bhonsle would not have thanked him for giving Pune the credit. I did not correct a king in his own hall."
5. **Landing timeline (L79 against L153; backlog N-11).** Trichinopoly fell in late March 1741. Ch 13 L135 to 137 has the Dutch bombarding Colachel in November 1740 and landing four hundred men in February 1741. L153's "Tomorrow, or the day after ... They would land" must go, as it does in the proposed ending. Ch 14 L5 ("wrestling with Chanda Sahib's men" when the Dutch came) should soften to "Chanda Sahib's leftover bands". The glossary (L21) and the character guide (L35) then need to match (FM-12).
6. **Geography.** L3 says the raids came "from the northeast", L13 says through the Shenkottai pass, L29 points at the "southeastern hills", L31 names Aramboli, L45 puts the fighting near Nagercoil, and L57 calls Shenkottai "the southeastern frontier". Ch 11 L131 and L435 say Chanda Sahib pushed "from the south". Shenkottai lies well north of Padmanabhapuram, at the Tamil end of the Aryankavu pass. The gap into Nanjanad and Nagercoil is Aramboli, which Ch 11 L149 correctly places on the south-eastern frontier. If the raids burned villages near Nagercoil, L13's pass is Aramboli, not Shenkottai. Settle one geography for Chs 11, 12 and 14 and verify it on a map.
