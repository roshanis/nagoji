## Verdict

| Measure | Call |
|:-|:-|
| Severity | **4 of 5** |
| Recommended intensity | **Deep**, but in three places only: the opening (L5 to 35), the blast through the parley (L77 to 167), and the coda (L183 to 185). The Mukkuvar scene (L37 to 59), the king and Ramayyan at the camp stool (L73 to 75) and the gun crew (L81 to 87) need a light touch. |
| Estimated word cut | **About 28%** (2,645 to about 1,900 words, after the short additions proposed below) |

This chapter holds some of the best battle writing in the book and some of the most machine-made. VOICE_BIBLE's touchstone T8 ("a square that turns is no longer a square. It is a crowd.") comes from here. So do Antony and the betel, the gunners slowed by heat, "where Ramayyan had quietly told them the enemy kept its teeth", Dhanaji "beside me, then ahead of me", and the king's "Let them taste the ash." Around them the chapter runs on scaffolding. There is a history briefing that ch11 to ch13 have already dramatised (L5 to 13). Then come three abstract hinge lines ("scattered strands", "a weight, not a collision", "no grand charge ... Instead") before a single trench appears. Each section is closed by a summary ("a terrifyingly efficient alliance", "a box where the walls were fire and the floor was water"). The blast is told in trailer beats ("For a heartbeat, nothing happened. / Then the world turned white."). The Dutch response is reframed three times in a dozen lines ("Not fleeing. Forming.", "They were not running to the sea. They were cutting their way out."). And the chapter ends three times (L147, L183, L185).

The register slips are the worst in Part III: shockwave, cyclone, beachhead, perimeter, casualty reports, cooking off, secondary explosions, powder room, hundred yards, "preparation meeting opportunity".

The 28% cut is well above the 5 to 10% the PLAN expects book-wide, and I have checked it against the risk of over-cutting. About 60% of it is repetition of other chapters or of this one: the backstory at L5 to 13 (ch11, ch13), the November bombardment at L7 (ch13 L135), Hackert and the sagging ramparts at L65 (ch13 L147 to 151), Ramayyan's résumé at L167, the parley gloss at L163, the recaps at L61, L147 and L183, and the moral at L185. No scene, plot beat or historical fact is lost. The fixes put back a few concrete things the chapter lacks: Nagoji counting under fire, the church as the magazine, the rice burning with the powder, and De Lannoy as a builder of forts.

Two decisions sit under the line edit and should be taken first, because several fixes depend on them. The first is whether the Dutch surrender on this beach or two days later (backlog N-14, which bears on L145, L151 and L185). The second is which horse Nagoji rides (N-03, which bears on L101, L129 and L135).

## Opening

Current first lines (L3 to 5): "In 1741, by the time we closed our ring around Colachel, we were already tired from another war. / The Dutch had not come when Travancore was fresh and waiting. They chose their moment while the Maharaja was far to the south ... They were striking a man who had only just pushed one invader back and had not yet found his footing again."

**Verdict: keep the first sentence; revise L5 to 23.** Both auditors agree the trouble starts at L5. What follows is nearly 400 words of briefing before the reader stands in a trench. Much of it is retold from ch11 to ch13: the English loans, the Elayadathu lever, the November bombardment. Three hinge lines then state the chapter's thesis ("a weight, not a collision", "no grand charge"). The briefing also contradicts Ch 12, where Chanda Sahib's capture frees the king to face the Dutch (backlog N-11), and it puts Anchuthengu at the southern tip (N-46).

**Proposed** (replaces L3 to 23; about 280 words against 511; every fact kept except the November bombardment, which Ch 13 L135 owns):

> In 1741, by the time we closed our ring around Colachel, we were already tired from another war.
>
> The Dutch had chosen their moment. They came while the Maharaja was far to the south, near the tip of the land, clearing out what Chanda Sahib's bands had left behind. The army had marched and bled along that front, coin had already been turned into borrowed muskets and English favours, and Ramayyan's palm leaves were full of debts.
>
> Riders galloped up and down the coast. Men who had thought they were going home from the southern campaign found themselves turned north instead. Guns that had faced Chanda Sahib's lines were dragged through wet fields and narrow village paths toward the sand where the Dutch had come ashore.
>
> Two years before Colachel, the company had decided to stop being a guest at our table. It took up the grievance of the Elayadathu princess at Kottarakkara and bought her pepper cheap under her own seal, and Desinganadu, Kochi, Purakkad and Thekkumkur, each with a grudge of its own, marched under the Dutch flag. For a few months blue coats sat in villages from Kottar to the edges of Colachel as if Padmanabhapuram were already within reach.
>
> The charge I had seen in my sleep did not come for months.
>
> Our lines ran round their camp from the beach on one side to the beach on the other, and we pinned them there against the sea. We dug trenches in the sandy soil and shored them with palm trunks and woven mats. We raised batteries that pounded their mud stockade day and night. We watched them through the haze of heat and salt spray, a blue and grey mass of men on a smaller patch of ground each week.

"Two years before Colachel" keeps the wording that settled backlog C12-02. "Padmanabhapuram" stays until the author rules on N-28 (see Chapter notes).

## Ending

Current last lines (L183 to 185):

> The siege of Colachel had ended not with the thunderclap alone, but with our charge through the smoke. The explosion had wounded them. The cavalry had finished it.
>
> But as I looked at the smoking ruin and the prisoners being led away, I knew the silence to come would be more dangerous than the noise. Now came the words. And words, in my experience, could be sharper than any sword.

**Verdict: revise (cut back to L181).** The chapter ends three times: the sunset recap at L147, the thesis at L183, the moral at L185. Each tells the reader what the scene has just shown, and two of them credit Nagoji's own charge. L185 is the STYLE_SHEET's banned shape: a last paragraph opening "But", an "I knew" foreshadow, a "Now came ..." kicker (also Ch 27 L231) and a proverb. VOICE_BIBLE Appendix A and OPENINGS_ENDINGS_MOTIFS both put the true ending at L181. Ch 15's first line ("The end came with a lie") already does the turn toward negotiation.

**Proposed** (L173 to 181 with the fixes above; ends on the king's line of dialogue):

> "Shall we finish them?" a captain asked.
>
> Marthanda Varma looked at the miserable huddle on the sand, at the ships turning away as the light went.
>
> "No," he said. "Dead men cannot sign treaties." He looked at De Lannoy. "And that one says he can build."
>
> He turned his horse away.
>
> "Let them simmer tonight," he said. "Let them taste the ash. Tomorrow, we will see what remains."

If the author wants Nagoji to have the last beat instead of the king, add one physical line after it, not a reflection: "I got down and walked my horse to the water to wash the blood off her chest." That uses the Kayal fix (N-03) and his habit of tenderness as chores (VOICE_BIBLE 3.5). My first choice is to end on the king.

## Chapter-specific notes

1. **The handoff from Ch 13, and how long the siege lasts.** Ch 13 ends at dawn with the drums ("The eve was over. / The storm was here."), and Ch 14 then rewinds months. L21 says "For weeks", but Ch 13 L137 has the encirclement begin in February and L79 puts the end on 10 August. Use "months" (done in the Opening proposal). Whether Ch 13's "eve" is the eve of the landing or of 10 August is backlog N-13. The L19 fix says "in my sleep", without naming the night, so it works either way.
2. **Chanda Sahib (N-11, FM-12).** Ch 12 L79 to 85 has him captured in spring 1741, which frees the king. L5's "wrestling with Chanda Sahib's men" becomes "clearing out what Chanda Sahib's bands had left behind", matching the Ch 12 audit. The glossary (L21) then needs to match.
3. **Anchuthengu (N-46).** It lies north of the capital, not "near the tip of the land". Dropped in the Opening proposal.
4. **Padmanabhapuram before its renaming (N-28).** L13 uses the name about a decade early. If the author adopts N-28, write "Kalkulam".
5. **Hackert and Rijtel against Donnadi (N-47, N-14).** Hackert sailed to Tuticorin (Ch 13 L151), not "north". L151 names Rijtel among the surviving officers, but Ch 15 L27 makes Donnadi "the senior surviving officer", and Rijtel never appears again. Either say in Ch 14 or 15 that Rijtel fell in the blast or the charge, or drop his name here. The L151 fix drops it.
6. **One surrender, not two (N-14).** Here the survivors throw down their arms (L145) and are "led away" (L185). In Ch 15 L5 to 11, De Lannoy carries terms for two days and the Dutch march out armed on the thirteenth. The L145 fix has them fall back into the stockade, and L185 is cut, so Ch 15 stands as written.
7. **The horse (N-03).** Kayal is a bay mare who "would carry me through Colachel and beyond" (Ch 7 L183). Here "My horse reared, screaming. I fought to control him" (L101). Recommended: "Kayal reared, screaming. I fought her down," with "her" kept at L129 and in the charge. Ch 16 L5's "the mount I had lost in the storm" is then settled in Ch 16, either as Kayal lost at Colachel or as Kayal surviving.
8. **Square, line and flank.** L117 calls the sortie a "ragged line"; L121 onward calls it a square. Keep the square, since T8 depends on it and it is plausible for a force cutting its way out through horse. Make L117 "The blue coats advanced through the smoke", and call the weak side a "face" at L127 (done in the table).
9. **Who rides in the charge.** L31 puts Madurai and Maratha riders on the wider tracks, L125 puts Maravar and Madurai horse on the left, and L137 says "Deccan riders". The L137 fix ("My riders") covers it. If the author wants the Maratha riders in the charge, add them at L125 instead.
10. **The king on the field.** Varma is not placed at the fight until "He looked at the king" (L171), yet he later turns his horse away. A small fix at L165: "I rode up to where Ramayyan stood beside the king's horse." Ch 16 L157 also gives the king a "bandage on his shoulder still stiff from Colachel", and nothing here wounds him (N-25). Either the king's arm is bound here in one clause, or Ch 16 changes.
11. **Nagoji's body.** Ch 15 L131 has "My ribs, bruised from the fighting and the hard turns of the charge", but the charge gives him no body at all. The borderline fix at L135 plants the bruise. That is the one addition I recommend beyond the cuts, because it also answers the holistic reader's point that the charge has no inner life.
12. **Language at the parley.** De Lannoy calls out in Portuguese (L151). Nagoji learned Portuguese in the Goa cellar (Ch 1), and Ramayyan answers in English on the page. One clause saying who carried the words, most naturally Nagoji, would be true to VOICE_BIBLE 3.7 and a quiet callback. The optional L163 replacement does this.
13. **Ramayyan's accusation is used twice.** L157, "You speak for men who burned our temples and took our people as slaves", comes back at Ch 15 L35 ("You burned our temples. You took our people as slaves."). Keep it in one chapter. Ch 15's use is stronger, because it answers "We have terms!", so L157 could become "Why should we listen?" alone or "Then speak."
14. **Echoes with Ch 15's second clash.** Ch 15 L141 to 145 has "A Dutch sergeant", a volley, and "It lasted minutes. It felt like an hour." With L131's "stretched like hours" cut, Ch 15 keeps its time line. Keep the sergeant in one chapter only (see Borderline, L131).
15. **Titles and forms.** L167 "The Diwan" against "Dalawa" from Ch 13 onward (N-42, FM-14). L79 "10th of August" against Ch 15's "twelfth of August" (fixed in the table). "The company" is lower case in L13 and L75 here but "Company" in Ch 15; add it to the house style list. The heading "The Siege of Colachel" and the file name "charge_at_colachel" still differ (N-43). With "There was no grand charge" gone, either title now fits the chapter.
16. **Typography.** Straight quotes at L71 to 75, L105, L119 and L153 to 161, curly elsewhere. The comma splice at L125 ("in the smoke, the Dutch square had a seam") is fixed in the table.
17. **History.** The magazine explosion on 10 August 1741, with the Dutch rice stores lost in the fire, matches the standard account, and the L107 fix now says so. The cavalry charge against the sortie is the novel's invention. That is fair in fiction, but the Author's Historical Note should not be read as vouching for it. Its line 15 ("crossed over during the Colachel campaign") still contradicts this chapter's surrender parley (SOURCE_OF_TRUTH item 2).
18. **Cross-chapter motifs touched here.** Water through a dam (L129; Ch 12 L13, Ch 23 L425), the "They were wrong" cadence (L109; Ch 12 L155, Ch 23 L203), words sharper than blades (L185; Ch 9 L254), "Now came ..." (L185; Ch 27 L231) and "For a heartbeat" (L97; Ch 13 L287) are all removed here. "Without ceremony" (L63) and "taste the ash" (L181) are this chapter's to keep or lose in the book-wide count.
