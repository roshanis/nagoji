## Verdict

| Measure | Call |
|:-|:-|
| Severity | **4 of 5**, at the top of the band. It is the densest chapter in the book by the REPETITION_AND_DENSITY tic index, but its scenes, jokes and people are sound, so the fix is cutting and re-voicing, not rebuilding. |
| Recommended intensity | **Deep**: line-level re-voicing of the proposal, the rite, the feast and the bedroom; a reframed prologue; a rebuilt ending. |
| Estimated word cut | **About 35%** (4,582 to about 2,950 words). About 28% if the author keeps the optional texture named at the end of this section. |

The chapter has a sound spine and a machine-written surface wherever feeling rises. Its best lines are authored and specific: the courtship smuggled into canal-rights letters and levy accounts, "Depends on the dream", the horse that does not like cannon smoke, Padmini's "full stomach, not an empty bed", the cowries, Dhanaji's three bribed boatmen and "the food is too wet", Lannoy unsure whether he is watching a military alliance, "terrified of a piece of string", Ramayyan's avial, "a guest who has been told he may stay past sunset", "All will call you loud", the mango-tree scar, the two names collapsing in bed, and the dead husband "longer than you have been on this coast". That is the register to edit toward.

Around those lines, the four big emotional scenes run on the book's worst template. The proposal (L77 to 151) trades balanced maxims (past and present, root and branch, own and serve), romance stock ("a ghost of a smile", "made my breath catch", "hung heavy in the air", "a breath I had not known she was holding") and a modern debate ("accept a woman as his equal", "patrilineal", "vulnerability", "heritage"). The tali rite (L212 to 274) explains every gesture, often three times, through "not X but Y" reveals. The feast (L278 to 306) becomes food writing and a garbled trivia lecture. The bedroom (L336 to 382) says "no more hiding" five ways. Then the chapter ends four times (L406 to 420).

Three structural faults travel with the prose and should be settled before the line edit:

1. The prologue (L3 to 37) presents as a first courtship an affair that Ch 16 and 17 have already made in Padmini's cellar. The rest of this chapter agrees with Ch 16 and 17, so the prologue is what changes (Chapter notes 1).
2. L390 "You are not a guest anymore" contradicts the title, L109, L312 to 314 and the book's own last page (Ch 28 L287, "a house that had taken me in as a guest").
3. The chapter ends four times.

The 35% cut is far above the PLAN's 5 to 10%, and that is deliberate. About two thirds of it is restatement in seven blocks: the guest point (L115, L121, L143 to 145), the rite's explanations (L220 to 262), the thieves and no-hiding run (L336 to 368), the feast menu and lecture (L278 to 306), the aftermath (L386 to 404) and the coda (L410 to 420). No scene, plot beat, named character or later callback is lost. I checked the lines later chapters lean on: Ch 26 L243 quotes L117 ("a corner of my house and a sliver of my heart"), Ch 28 L263 remembers the western verandah, and Ch 28 L287 closes the book on the guest idea. All three survive. If the author wants a lighter edit, keeping the full sadya menu, a line of the priest's liturgy, L304 and L314 as written brings the cut to about 28%.

## Chapter-specific notes (continuity and history)

1. **Two framings of the romance (settle first).** Ch 16 L90 to 147 and Ch 17 L229 to 277 make Revathi and Nagoji lovers in Padmini's cellar soon after Colachel, and Ch 18 L203 gives them a hand-brush on the verandah after the Dutch bow. The body of this chapter agrees (L232, L242, L272, L336 to 356). Only the prologue (L3 to 37) reads as a first courtship ("things shifted", "circling each other", "the gap between us was too wide"). Keep the cellar as canon. The L3, L17 and L35 fixes turn the prologue into lovers whose secret has been noticed. The pattern auditor's alternative, removing the cellar references, would break Ch 16 and 17.
2. **Timeline.** "Some months after Colachel" (August 1741) at L3, plus the festival and two months, puts the wedding roughly a year after the battle. That fits the Colachel scars "still pink" (L372) and L17's reference to the Dutch envoys of Ch 18. It does not fit L163 "years ago", which is cut. Revathi's "more grey" (L65) and "You are older" (L374) are fine, measured from Ch 9.
3. **The rite (history, the author's decision).** In 18th-century Nair custom the *tali-kettu* was, as I understand the sources, usually a rite for girls before puberty, and an adult union was *sambandham*, commonly sealed by the man's gift of cloth (*pudavakoda*, also called *pudamuri*). A Sanskrit rite at a sacred fire, with an adult widow receiving a tali from her new husband and three knots allegorised, reads as a generic South Indian Brahmin wedding. Verify against the author's sources. Three things make this worth a decision. Padmini taught Nagoji the word *sambandham* at Ch 8 L151, and this chapter never uses it. The priest's "Tie it, and she is yours" (L224) contradicts Ch 8 L159 ("He does not own her") and Revathi at L105. And the green-cloth summons (L41 to 47) already inverts the cloth custom, so the plainest period fix is for Nagoji to give her a cloth in return at the rite. If the author keeps the tali, cut "she is yours" (Confirmed) and consider one line making the public rite Revathi's choice. L354, now cut, gave the reason: "the knowledge of everyone who matters".
4. **The dawn custom.** Early European accounts of Malabar describe the *sambandham* husband visiting at night and leaving before dawn. If the author's sources agree, the proposed L390 ("You do not have to be gone before the house wakes ... Let my uncles watch you walk out of it") turns the aftermath into Revathi breaking custom for him. That is stronger than "You are not a guest anymore", and it leaves the title intact.
5. **Revathi's standing.** L81 has her uncles bringing proposals, and L101 has her call herself "the matriarch of Velinadu" who commands men. In a Nair house the *karanavar*, the senior man of the matriline, usually managed the property. Ch 9 establishes her as the voice of Velinadu, so this is not a slop fix, but the proposed L101 drops "matriarch" to avoid the question. The author may want one line in Book 3 on why the uncles do not rule.
6. **The sambar lecture (L300).** The popular story names sambar for Chhatrapati Sambhaji, cooked in the Thanjavur Maratha court kitchen. It is a modern legend, and "took refuge in the south" belongs to Rajaram, who held out at Jinji from 1689. Nagoji, of all people, would not take either from a Travancore minister without comment. Cut. If the author wants a Maratha beat at the feast, Nagoji can gloss the foreign dish by his own, as VOICE_BIBLE recommends: "The *sambar* was a wetter cousin of my mother's *amti*." It also answers Dhanaji's "The food is too wet." Optional; verify *amti* for a Nashik kitchen of the 1720s.
7. **The avial story (L286).** The usual legends credit Bhima, or a royal kitchen with too many vegetables. Padmini's claim for Ramayyan is a tall tale told as a joke, which is fine, and Ramayyan's deadpan keeps it light.
8. **Dhanaji and the king's knife.** This is Dhanaji's last scene in the book (no later chapter names him), and the conch knife goes north with him as a series thread (OPENINGS_ENDINGS_MOTIFS). The king gave it at Ch 17 L173 with "Keep it close. Let every man who sees it understand whose work you do." Nagoji gives it away without a thought for Marthanda Varma. Dhanaji's "I will not carry a foreign king's treasure" half covers it. One clause from Nagoji would close the gap, for example: "The king would ask where it had gone. I would tell him." Also fix L191 (Confirmed). VOICE_BIBLE continuity note 4 (how Dhanaji reached this coast) is still open and touches L171.
