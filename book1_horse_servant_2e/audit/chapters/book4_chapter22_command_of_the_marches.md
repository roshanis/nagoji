## Verdict

Two writers share this chapter. The first wrote the approach to the shrine and most of the aftermath talk. Five of the VOICE_BIBLE's model lines come from those passages: T12 (L71 to 75), the single beat in the body (L77), partial comprehension (L87), pain noticed after the fact (L257) and the self-portrait joke (L399). The "Steps" / "The gods are clumsy hosts" exchange (L315 to 319) is some of the best dialogue in the book. The second writer wrote a screen thriller: "Count the exits", "deer in torchlight", "He was not most men", the dancers, "pupils blown", "Then training took over", "the thin line between life and death". By raw hits per thousand words, Ch 22 is the densest chapter in the book (REPETITION_AND_DENSITY: 40.1). It has nine to eleven "not X, but Y" frames and 52 paragraphs of six words or fewer, bunched into cascades at L57 to 73, L99 to 109, L139 to 149, L193 to 209 and L219 to 237.

It gets a 4 rather than a 5 because the scene logic holds and its best passages are among the book's best. It gets a 4 rather than a 3 because the fight and the last forty lines read as machine-made, line after line.

**This ruling cuts less than the pattern auditor asked for.** I defend 23 of its flags. Most are lines VOICE_BIBLE already holds up as models, or plain facts and short dialogue the story needs. The ruling also **adds what both auditors missed**:
- Nagoji has fought chavers before (Ch 8 L9), so "I had heard of such men in stories" (L119) is false.
- The priest who is sent away at L263 to 265 still has his arm around the heir at L311.
- Nobody goes to the ten-year-old heir after the killing, although Ch 24 L387 remembers him clinging to his uncle's leg.
- The king's extermination order at L281 is given again, in full, at Ch 23 L495 to 521.

**Three repairs do more than any single line edit**, and the confirmed table builds them in:
1. **Let Nagoji count.** When he is afraid, he counts (VOICE_BIBLE T2), and this chapter never lets him. R1 replaces the "Old habit / Count the exits / Everything seemed ordinary" cascade (L57 to 67) with a real count. The assassin then becomes "a third temple man ... I had not counted him". The same fix removes the duplicated courtyard inventory and the confusion between two "younger" temple men.
2. **Answer the dead assistant.** He takes the first blade (L105), lies with his eyes open (L247), and then vanishes. L345 even credits his blade to Nagoji. R2 turns Nagoji's stock "Not fast enough" into a look at the body, and the king orders rice for the boy's mother. Ramayyan names him again at L345.
3. **Put the cavalryman in the fight.** Nagoji hands his talwar to a guard at the foot of the stair (L37). That pays off the chaver's empty grab at his belt (L181), explains why the king's bodyguard is unarmed, and lets his sword-trained hand make the kill in its own way (L219 to 223, "The talwar had taught my hand to draw an edge").

### Rewrite R1 (replaces L57 to L67)

> I stayed under the bel tree and counted what the courtyard held, as I did in any place I had not chosen. One stair, down to the guards. One door, to a side room. The old priest, going between the side room and the sanctum with his ghee and wicks. His assistant by the mandapa steps with a torch. A dog asleep against the wall, twitching in some chase dream.
>
> Then a third temple man came out of the side room. I had not counted him.

This version works because a count is how Nagoji takes hold of a place (T2), and a man missing from the count is how he would register danger before he could name it. Counting the side-room door means the reader has already seen where the threat will come from, which is fair play. The paragraph removes six flagged items at once and settles which temple man is which.

### Rewrite R2 (replaces L285 to L291)

> “You moved fast,” he said.
>
> “Not fast enough.” I was looking at the temple assistant.
>
> The king looked too. “Find out his name,” he said. “His mother will have his rice for as long as she lives.”

L293 follows unchanged, without "finally". The king speaks in short orders, as he does at his best. The rice is both a kindness and the price of the "slipped on the steps" cover story, which leaves no room for a dead temple servant. Both readings suit this king.

## Opening

Current first lines (L3 to 13):

> The king liked to meet his gods without the court watching.
>
> Not at the great temples where priests and courtiers watched every gesture, but at an old Shiva shrine ... older than the oldest temples, older than the first palm-leaf records, older than any king brave enough to pretend the coast belonged to him.
>
> “If I breathe too often under carved ceilings,” he had said once, “I forget what rain smells like on bare rock.”
>
> That night he and the heir wanted to climb the hill.
>
> Not for ceremony, not for show, but to stand before a black *lingam* and remember that some things do not belong to a crown. ...
>
> We rode without a procession.

**Verdict: revise.** L3 and L7 are good. L3 is plain and tells us something about the king in eleven words. L7 is better still: the king gives his reason as a smell. Between them, L5 repeats L3 as a "Not at X, but at Y" correction (the chapter-opening tic this book repeats in eight chapters) and piles up three "older than" clauses. L11 does a second "not ..., not ..., but" six lines later and ends on an abstraction. The reader then gets Ramayyan's objection three times (L11 summary, L15 to 19 scene, L21 summary) before anyone climbs a step.

**Proposed** (L3 to L15, about 110 words instead of 200; then continue with L17, L19 and L23, cutting L21):

> The king liked to meet his gods without the court watching. His favourite was an old Shiva shrine on a rocky hill a little inland, with mango trees crowding its walls. Men in the fishing villages said it was older than any king brave enough to pretend the coast belonged to him.
>
> “If I breathe too often under carved ceilings,” he had said once, “I forget what rain smells like on bare rock.”
>
> That night he wanted to climb the hill with the heir. We rode out with no procession, only Ramayyan and a handful of his guards.
>
> Ramayyan had wanted guards on the hill itself, a few trusted Nair blades, a Marakkar with a keen eye. “You remember the festival night when you fell on the temple stones,” he said. “Not every knife announces itself.” Marthanda Varma listened, then shook his head.

From L39 on, the arrival at the shrine is good: "barely the size of a Deccan village house", the roots like knuckles, and the heir praying "a fraction too quickly". The chapter picks up there.

## Ending

Current last lines (L403 to L409):

> As I rode north some weeks later, standard snapping above my cavalry column, shoulder still aching faintly in the evenings, I thought of the temple stone, of the thin line between life and death measured by inches of steel and a man's decision to step in instead of aside.
>
> The Kayamkulam frontier waited.
>
> So did whatever knives the old houses there had honed in the dark.
>
> But for the first time since Goa, I felt not like wreckage caught between currents, but like part of a river that had chosen its course.

**Verdict: rewrite (by cutting).** The chapter ends three times: the toast (L385 to 393), Padmini (L395 to 401) and the ride north (L403 to 409), and each ending is weaker than the one before. The last one breaks most of the STYLE_SHEET's ending rules at once. It re-narrates the fight as a reflection, uses "the thin line between life and death", falls into the "X waited. So did Y." cadence, opens its last paragraph with "But", reaches for "for the first time since", frames the close as "not like X, but like Y", narrates his own growth, and uses the chapter's third river image. Ch 23 opens on the frontier itself, so the chapter needs no bridge to get there.

**Proposed** (L395 to 401, unchanged except as noted; cut L403 to 409; keep L385 to 389 without Nagoji's reply, as in the confirmed table):

> When word of my new command reached Padmini, she came to the fort with her stick tapping more sharply than usual.
>
> “So,” she said, looking me up and down. “You finally decided which current to trust. I hope your horse can swim.”
>
> “They can,” I said. “I am less sure about their rider.”
>
> “You managed a chaver,” she said. “The frontier will be easier. At least there, the men who want to kill you will have to ride a little before they try.”

This ends on a line of dialogue that is dry and funny and points at the danger to come. It is also the ending that VOICE_BIBLE Appendix A and OPENINGS_ENDINGS_MOTIFS both find already sitting in the draft. Unlike OPENINGS_ENDINGS_MOTIFS, I keep L397 and L399 (see Defended and note 21). If the L377 horses line is adopted and the author wants one last act of work rather than a joke, add a final line: "The next morning I was down at the horse lines before the grooms, choosing." I prefer ending on Padmini.

## Chapter-specific notes (continuity and history)

1. **Three temple knife attacks (backlog N-17; OPENINGS_ENDINGS_MOTIFS item 2).** The festival attack at Ch 21 L193, this chapter's shrine attack and the Padmanabhaswamy attack at Ch 23 L417 to 449 share their moves. Nagoji catches the wrist (Ch 21 and Ch 22). The king goes down on spilled oil (Ch 21 and Ch 22). A chaver goes for the heir, who freezes (Ch 22 and Ch 23). The king bleeds (all three). Ch 24 L387 and L399 remember a mixture of Ch 22 and Ch 23. This chapter plants its oil better (L95, then L165), so it should keep the oil, and the author should consider changing how the king falls in the Ch 21 memory. Ch 23 needs a different target or method, or it should say plainly that this is the second chaver in a month.
2. **The extermination order is given twice (L281; Ch 23 L495 to 521).** The L281 fix holds it back for Ch 23 and has the king ask instead who let the chaver into the side room, which is the question a reader will be asking. Ch 23's "Chavers of our own" then carries the word into the king's mouth for the first time.
3. **Three renunciations of the north (OPENINGS_ENDINGS_MOTIFS item 4).** The L373 and L377 fixes keep Ch 22 about the frontier alone. The letter to his brother (Ch 19) and the burned letter (Ch 25) keep the family break and the political break.
4. **Nagoji has fought chavers before.** Ch 8 L9 has him leading cavalry against "a band of chavers" on a paddy bund, so L119's "I had heard of such men in stories" cannot stand. The L269 gloss uses that earlier fight instead. It also settles backlog C10-41 for this chapter: the word is glossed at first use in Ch 8, felt here, and the Ch 23 L427 gloss can go.
5. **Ch 19 L181 is a premature callback (backlog RV-01).** It remembers "a dying chaver's wrist while a king drove steel through his throat". Here Nagoji makes the cut, and this chapter comes later. Fix it in Ch 19, not here.
6. **Nobody goes to the heir after the killing.** The boy is ten (Ch 17 L9). He is left behind a pillar from L113 to L311, and the priest holding him leaves at L265 but is still holding him at L311. Ch 24 L387 remembers "the wide-eyed boy who had clung to his uncle's leg". Suggested, at L271: "He glanced toward the pillar and held out his arm. The boy came and sat against his side, one fist in his cloth, and did not let go." L311 then reads "and the heir pressed against the king's side". That adds about 30 words and brings the king closer to the boy than the one "his child" line at L91 ever did.
7. **The dead assistant** gets an answer twice (R2 and the L345 fix). Ramayyan's "We will clear this. Quietly." (L323) then covers a body the king has already provided for.
8. **The unarmed bodyguard.** The L37 fix has Nagoji leave his talwar at the foot of the stair. Verify that weapons were barred from a Kerala temple courtyard (they generally were inside the *nalambalam*). If so, the chaver's hidden dagger becomes a second sacrilege, which the priest's horror at L261 can carry without comment.
9. **Shoulders.** The king's shoulder hangs low at L251 because he took the chaver's surge on it at L215, so that is consistent, and the holistic reader's objection there does not hold. The real problem is Nagoji's own shoulder, which is "popped" (L175), "dislocated" (L257) and "bruised" (L311). The L175 and L257 fixes make it a bad bruise throughout.
10. **Relationship and age of the heir.** He is the king's nephew (Ch 17 L9; Ch 23 L521 "my nephew"), and the L91 fix removes "his child". The historical heir, Karthika Thirunal Rama Varma, was born in 1724 and was a young man by the Kayamkulam wars (backlog N-29). The book's boy of ten is an authorial choice. Note it only.
11. **No year anchor (backlog CL-06).** Ch 22 has no date, and Ch 20 to 24 have none between them. If the author wants one here, the L357 offer or the L403 ride (if any of it is kept) can carry "in the dry season of 1742". Verify against the Kayamkulam campaign dates the book uses.
12. **Kayamkulam and the Dutch (L349).** "wonder whether they might trim it while Dutch eyes are elsewhere". Historically the VOC backed Kayamkulam against Travancore in the early 1740s (verify), and Ch 23 L371 has the frontier houses taking "foreign money". Suggest: "men there who take Dutch money and wonder whether they might trim my strength with it."
13. **Where the Eight Houses were (L269).** The historical Ettuveetil Pillamar held villages around Thiruvananthapuram, well south of Kayamkulam, and the glossary (L10) ties them to temple revenues there. Ch 21 L62 calls them "Eight Houses of the Marches" and says "Broken formally, roots remain". The book's relocation of their survivors to the northern frontier is workable if it is said once, for example: "Kin of the old Eight Houses, fled north to the Kayamkulam side." Keep the glossary, the character guide and Ch 21 to 23 consistent.
14. **Chavers, historically,** belong to Valluvanad's feud with the Zamorin at the Mamankam at Tirunavaya, in the north. L119's "Warriors from the north" was the one accurate fact in that paragraph. If the author wants to keep any of it, keep that phrase in the L269 gloss ("the chavers of the north"). A chaver sworn against Marthanda Varma is the novel's own extension, and a plausible one.
