# Chapter 2 Audit: The Slave Ship South

Source: `book1_horse_servant/book1_chapter02_slave_ship_south.md` (first edition, frozen; 131 lines, 1,897 words by `wc`). Line numbers refer to that file.

Adjudicated from two independent reports (a pattern auditor with 62 flags and a holistic reader with 27 suspect passages), checked against a full read of the chapter, Chapter 1, the opening of Chapter 3, `STYLE_SHEET.md`, `VOICE_BIBLE.md`, `OPENINGS_ENDINGS_MOTIFS.md`, `REGISTER_AND_ANACHRONISM.md` and `REVIEW_BACKLOG.md`. Duplicates across the two reports are merged into single rows.

## Verdict

| | |
|---|---|
| Severity | **3 of 5**: a noticeable pattern throughout, not pervasive |
| Recommended intensity | **Medium**: cut the tics, tighten five blocks, and re-voice two passages (line 25 and lines 97 to 99) at line level |
| Estimated word cut | **About 20 percent** (1,897 to about 1,490 words when every confirmed fix and recommended borderline swap is applied; see the appendix) |

The chapter has two voices. In dialogue and hard detail it is Nagoji: João's tap-skip-cuff rhythm, matchcords at the musketeers' belts, a stern "like a little fortress of carved wood and painted saints", "Horses would hate this", "The sahib still counts chances". The connective narration between those moments is where nearly all the machine patterns sit: paragraphs that close on a maxim or mood line, "not X, but Y" corrections, three-beat fragments, weight images, and a closing run of three kickers. The dialogue loses almost nothing in this edit.

The cut is higher than the book-wide 5 to 10 percent target, but it is concentrated in five blocks: the Duarte beat (lines 31 to 41), the walk to the harbour (43 to 49), the essay on shipboard time (97), the interior commentary after Keshavrao's promise (119 to 121) and the ending (127 to 131). Outside those blocks the edit is mostly word swaps.

The biggest single lever, which both auditors point toward: the chapter keeps *telling* us Nagoji counts ("the part of my mind that kept accounts", "The sahib still counts chances") and never lets him do it. Chapter 1 set up the exact things he would count here ("How many steps from the stair to the courtyard above", ch1:93). Several fixes below replace an abstraction with one of his numbers, which removes the modern voice without touching plot.

## Opening

Current first lines (3):

> They woke us under cover of bells. The great church above rang for early Mass, a flood of sound rolling down through stone into the cells. I counted the strokes. When the last echo faded, bolts scraped at the dungeon door.

Verdict: **keep the first sentence, fix the next three.** The hook is good and in scene, and "I counted the strokes" carries his counting over from Chapter 1. Three problems undercut it. The bolts move only after the bells stop, so the bells cover nothing. "A flood of sound rolling down through stone" is generic. And "cells" contradicts Chapter 1's single shared cellar. The fix in the table (sound "came down through the stone into the cellar"; bolts scrape "at the thirtieth, with the bells still going") keeps every idea and makes the idiom true.

João's entrance (5 to 13) is the strongest stretch of the opening and should stay almost as written: the humming, the tap-skip-cuff list, "marata", "The Viceroy has found a use for you." Only the bells-tune sentence (9) and the "cell wall" (11) change.

The opening's real gap is Keshavrao. He is the emotional centre of this chapter and the next, he never appears in Chapter 1, and here he arrives as "familiar" on a chain that has not yet been described. We learn he is young only at line 121, which this audit cuts. The line 17 fix gives him one clause of introduction at first sight and puts the chaining first.

## Ending

Current last lines (127 to 131):

> His fingers closed briefly over my bandaged hand. Neither of us mentioned that promises made in chains are the easiest to break.
>
> Above, the timbers creaked in a new rhythm. The ship rolled more sharply, the water slapping her sides with a sound that had teeth. Somewhere, a sailor cursed. Somewhere else, a prayer rose.
>
> The Arabian Sea was beginning to remind us who owned this ship.

Verdict: **rewrite.** Three closers run back to back: a maxim (127), a mirrored pair (129) and a personified moral (131). Beyond the slop there is a structural reason to cut line 129 entirely, not trim it as OPENINGS_ENDINGS_MOTIFS proposes. Chapter 3 opens on exactly this beat ("The first warning ... was the change in the way the ship moved", ch3:3; "that rhythm snapped. / The roll sharpened.", ch3:5-7), and ch3:5 needs time to have passed ("Even in chains I had learned the rhythm of this hull"). Ending Chapter 2 on the roll changing tells the storm's onset twice and makes Chapter 3's first line redundant. It would also make three storm-forecast endings in a row with Chapter 1, when the style sheet saves the storm for Chapter 13. The sailors' storm talk at 103 to 105 is plant enough.

Proposed ending (replaces 127 to 131):

> His fingers closed over mine and held on.
>
> That night I scratched the twelfth mark into the plank above my head. Keshavrao was asleep, and the chain that ran between us lifted a little with every breath he took.

It ends on an image: the chain that binds them, moved by the boy's breathing. It gives the voyage a length, puts Nagoji's counting on the page one last time, and leaves Chapter 3's "One night" free to begin the storm. "Held on" is the grip that fails in Chapter 3. This ending depends on the marks introduced at 97 to 99. If the author rejects the marks, the cut-only fallback is to end on "His fingers closed over mine and held on."

## Chapter-specific notes

1. **"Sri Lanka" (55)** is a P1 anachronism (the name dates from 1972). Use "Lanka" in a prisoner's mouth. The same fix is needed at 11:121, 11:129 and 13:105, where "Ceylon" suits European and court speech.
2. **Nashik (121).** Chapter 1 makes Nashik his landlocked home village (ch1:7), and no Portuguese guns were fielded there. The 1737 to 1739 fighting was in the Konkan (Salsette, Thana, Vasai, Chaul). Cutting line 121 removes the error. If any of it is kept, say "the Konkan".
3. **Chaul (89)** matches ch1:55 (Kanka carried him "through musket fire near Chaul"), so leave it. The main Maratha push on Chaul came in 1739 to 1740, after his capture, but skirmishing near Chaul on the Angre border before 1738 is plausible. No change needed.
4. **Keshavrao is introduced nowhere before line 17.** The character guide calls him "A younger comrade from the Konkan campaign"; Chapter 3 calls him "the boy" and gives him "thin hands". Give his age once, at first sight (the line 17 fix; the number is the author's choice).
5. **Leg irons.** Line 15 takes the ankle shackles off and nothing puts them back, but Chapter 3 has "iron biting into ankles and wrists" (ch3:27) and "The chain still linking his ankles" (ch3:177). The line 67 fix re-applies them in the hold.
6. **Chapter 3 misquotes line 117.** ch3:225 remembers "his quiet 'with you, sahib' in the dark", but here he says "I will follow wherever you jump, sahib." Align ch3:225 with this line when Chapter 3 is edited.
7. **Chapter 3 repeats the ship-break exchange.** ch2:113 ("will the ship break?") and ch3:39-41 ("if the ship breaks...") give Keshavrao the same question twice. Chapter 2's version, with "All ships break", is stronger. Flag it for the Chapter 3 audit.

## Appendix: the chapter with all fixes applied

This is for reading, to check that the voice survives the cut, not for pasting. It applies every confirmed fix and the recommended borderline swaps. The invented numbers (thirty strokes, nineteen steps, twenty masts, five to a pen, ninth and twelfth marks, thirty cruzados, Keshavrao's age) are placeholders for the author. About 1,490 words.

> They woke us under cover of bells. The great church above rang for early Mass, and the sound came down through the stone into the cellar. I counted the strokes. At the thirtieth, with the bells still going, bolts scraped at the dungeon door.
>
> João entered with three guards at his back.
>
> “Up,” he shouted in rough Konkani, the vowels rounded by a Portuguese tongue. “Those marked go to the docks.”
>
> He moved down the line with a leather strip of symbols, tapping one man, skipping two, cuffing another awake, all the while humming a hymn under his breath.
>
> When he reached me he did not bother to look at his strip. He knew where I sat. He had spent too many hours standing over me, hands busy with iron and rope.
>
> “On your feet, marata,” he said. “The Viceroy has found a use for you.”
>
> The guards unlocked the shackles at my ankles. Without the iron my legs came up so light they frightened me. It took me two tries to stand, and I made myself walk without a limp.
>
> They chained us in a line, six men to a length of iron, wrists manacled, collars clamped around our necks. My collar was greasy inside from the necks of the men who had worn it before me.
>
> Two places ahead of me on the chain was Keshavrao, who had ridden behind me through the Konkan fighting and was not yet twenty. His narrow shoulders were stiff, and the Portuguese had hacked his black hair short. His right hand twitched at his side, reaching for a sword hilt that was not there.
>
> “Rao,” I said softly.
>
> His head came up, but he did not look back. A guard walked between us, club in hand.
>
> I counted the steps as we climbed: nineteen, in two turns. At each turn the air was fresher, and the cellar stink gave way to lamp oil, then baking bread, then the sea.
>
> We came out into a courtyard of whitewashed walls under a glaring sky, and for a while I saw nothing but white. The first things I made out were the musketeers along the walls, hats tilted, matchcords coiled at their belts.
>
> Father Duarte stood near the gate.
>
> He was dressed for travel, a broad hat shading his grey eyes, a leather satchel over one shoulder. When our line of prisoners shuffled past, his gaze moved along the chain until it found me, and stayed there.
>
> His jaw tightened. His hand rose and made the sign of the cross toward me, brow to breast and shoulder to shoulder, taking the time a priest takes over the dead.
>
> He said nothing. When the guards pushed us toward the gate he turned and went in through a side door of the fortress.
>
> I set his face down in my accounts, beside the hand that had closed on João's wrist at the brazier. Men who hesitate are men who can be used.
>
> They moved us through narrow streets where the balconies almost touched above our heads. Goan Catholics in clean cotton stepped aside as we passed, hands over mouths and noses. A few Hindu merchants watched from their doorways and said nothing. One of them was counting us; I saw his lips move.
>
> The smell of salt grew stronger. So did the stink of tar, fish and unwashed sailors.
>
> At the harbour I began to count masts and gave it up past twenty. Ropes creaked, gulls screamed, and the heave of water against hulls kept up a low groan.
>
> One ship loomed closer than the rest, her hull black and high, her stern built up like a little fortress of carved wood and painted saints. Men moved along her rails, hauling on lines, shouting in Portuguese and Konkani and a tongue I did not know. On the wharf below her a platform had been erected, and on it a clerk checked lists against the lines of chained prisoners.
>
> “To Angola,” someone whispered behind me. “Africa.”
>
> “Not this one,” said another voice, older and bitter. “The Jesuits need bodies for their lands to the south. Some place beyond Lanka. They will work us until we forget our own names.”
>
> João tugged at our chain. “Faster,” he grunted. “The tide will not wait for a marata.”
>
> We were herded up a gangplank slick with sea and tar. The ship towered over us and stank of bilge water. As my bare feet touched the wood, the planks flexed under my weight, shifting as the water lifted and dropped the hull.
>
> Horses would hate this.
>
> Sailors darted around us on deck, some of them Habshis like the Siddi's men at Janjira, some pale Europeans whose faces burned red in the sun. Above, canvas cracked as it caught the breeze.
>
> They drove us below.
>
> The hold had been fitted for us. Shelves ran along both sides, split into pens by upright planks, and iron rings had been bolted into the beams overhead to take our chains. They put five men to a pen, so that none of us could lie flat, and closed leg irons on us again before they left. Someone had scratched prayers into the wood above my head, in a script I could not read.
>
> Keshavrao and I ended in the same section by accident or indifference. When the guards moved on, rattling chains and cursing, he turned his head at last.
>
> “Sahib,” he whispered.
>
> I had never liked that word from him. In camp I had made him use my name. This time I let it stand.
>
> “Rao,” I said. “You are still alive.”
>
> He gave a small, breathless laugh that turned into a cough. “For now.”
>
> We spoke little at first. Chains scraped. Men shifted against their cramps. At the far end of the hold someone began to chant in Konkani, near enough to my own tongue that I could follow the prayer, his voice rising and falling with the creak of the hull.
>
> Through a hatch far above, a square of light showed strips of sky. A gull crossed that square, a white streak against blue, and the ship began to move.
>
> “Do you know where they send us?” Keshavrao asked after a while.
>
> “South,” I said. “That much is certain.”
>
> “I heard one of the guards say they need men to cut cane,” he muttered. “Fields of sugar, as tall as a man. Sun that burns the skin off your back. The priest promised them that work in distant lands counts the same as service to their God. We will sweat for their salvation.”
>
> In camp he had been the one who joked, who found some rough song to carry us through long marches. I remembered him riding bareheaded near Chaul, singing over the crack of muskets, daring the Portuguese to shoot the song out of his mouth.
>
> “We are not there yet,” I said. “As long as we move, there is a chance.”
>
> He shifted his weight, chains clinking. “The sahib still counts chances.”
>
> “It is what I know.”
>
> They brought us rice and dried fish twice a day. Each evening, when the hatch opened for the second meal, I scratched a mark on the plank above my head with the edge of my wrist iron, under the prayers someone else had left there. The water was stale and tasted of the cask. Men fell sick quickly in the close, damp air. Coughs turned wet. Wounds from the dungeon festered. By the ninth mark the little space that each of us had claimed had narrowed, as the weaker sagged against the stronger.
>
> Sometimes, when the hatches stayed open a little longer, Portuguese voices drifted down from above.
>
> “Wind is with us,” one man said once. “If it holds we round Comorim before the worst of the storms.”
>
> “If the storms do not find us anyway,” another replied. “This run is cursed. The last ship took lightning in the mast. They say God sends warnings.”
>
> “God sends opportunity,” a third voice cut in, amused. “These bodies in the hold are silver if they live to reach land. Thirty cruzados a head.” He priced us as if we were sacks of pepper. “Pray for their health, not their souls.”
>
> Their laughter filtered down with the light.
>
> Below deck Keshavrao shivered.
>
> “If there is a storm,” he said, voice low, “will the ship break?”
>
> “All ships break,” I said. “The question is when and how.”
>
> He was silent for a time. Then he said, “If the chains break too, I will follow wherever you jump, sahib.”
>
> I reached across the narrow gap between us and rested my bandaged hand on his forearm.
>
> “If there is a chance,” I said, “I will not leave you.”
>
> His fingers closed over mine and held on.
>
> That night I scratched the twelfth mark into the plank above my head. Keshavrao was asleep, and the chain that ran between us lifted a little with every breath he took.
