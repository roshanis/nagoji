# Nagoji Sawant: Voice Bible

*Horse of the Servant*, second edition. The reference for the line edit.

Built from the frozen first edition in `book1_horse_servant/`. Line numbers refer to those files as they stand. Citation format: `ch07 L29-31` means `book2_chapter07_horses_in_wet_sand.md`, lines 29 to 31. The chapter key is Appendix E. Every passage in blockquotes is quoted exactly from the source.

`STYLE_SHEET.md` says what to cut. This document says what Nagoji sounds like when he is right, what to write when a cut leaves a hole, and what to protect so the edit does not flatten him.

## 0. How to use this

- Before each editing session, reread the fifteen touchstones in section 2. They take ten minutes and they reset the ear.
- Put every doubtful sentence through the three questions below.
- Section 6 is the checklist. Section 6b is the list of things not to cut.
- The appendices are lookup tables: endings that already exist a few lines early (A), repeated motifs (B), out-of-period words (C), continuity snags noticed while reading (D), chapter key (E).

**The three questions**

1. Could Nagoji touch, count, ride, eat, price or smell the thing this sentence is about?
2. Would he say it aloud to Dhanaji at a cook fire, or shout it at a recruit on the sand?
3. Has the sentence before it already shown this?

Yes, yes, no: keep it. Any other answer: rework it or cut it.

## 1. The voice in one paragraph

Nagoji writes like a cavalry officer keeping a campaign book he does not expect anyone to read. He sees footing before scenery, prices before beauty, hands before faces. When he is afraid he counts: guards, shifts, keys, paces, days of sail. He is funny by making things smaller and more exact, never grander. His tenderness goes to horses and comes out as chores: rubbing down, burying, scrubbing. He judges foreigners by their tools and habits (a priest's calluses, a gaoler's sausage breath, an officer's clean cuffs) and rarely by labels. He speaks Marathi, gets by in trade Konkani, learns Malayalam slowly and says so, and hears Portuguese as the language of the torture room. He almost never names what he feels. He tells you what his throat did, and then what he did next.

**What the numbers say.** Compare the fifteen touchstones below (1,271 words) with the eight contrast passages in section 4 (554 words). Neither set is long-winded: the touchstones average 11.4 words a sentence, the contrast passages 9.9. The difference is where the short sentences go and what the nouns are. In the contrast passages 43 percent of sentences are five words or fewer, against 23 percent in the touchstones, and they carry two and a half times the abstract nouns (2.3 per hundred words against 0.9: *anticipation, responsibility, foundation, truth, something, weight*). The fifteen touchstones hold three similes between them. The real voice is not terse. It is concrete, and its short sentences carry facts, not verdicts. (A rough count by script, with a short word list; indicative, not a measurement.)

## 2. Touchstones: fifteen passages the whole edition should sound like

Each passage is quoted exactly. "Why it is alive" names the moves. "Steal" is the move to reuse elsewhere. "Soft spot" marks the one phrase an editor could still tighten, so nobody treats the touchstones as untouchable.

### T1. "Stubborn is better than broken" (ch01 L17-23, 148 words)

> My gaze stayed on him without seeming to. That habit predated the Portuguese by years. As a boy in the Deccan I had learned to sit in the corner of a fort courtyard and see everything, from the loosened strap on a trooper's saddle to the way a sardar's hand tightened on his sword hilt at the mention of a rival. Here in this cellar I turned the same skill on other men.
>
> The gaoler, João, moved beside the priest. He was a broad man whose belly strained his leather belt, a man who had learned to wrap cruelty in jokes. He smelled of garlic sausage and chapel incense, an unholy combination. When he passed my place he tapped my shoulder with his boot, almost companionable.
>
> “Este,” he said in Portuguese, “this one is stubborn.”
>
> I kept my face blank. Stubborn is better than broken in any language.

Why it is alive:
- He tells you how he watches before he watches. A boy in a fort courtyard learned to see the loosened strap and the sardar's hand on the hilt. The observer has a history.
- One smell judges a whole regime: garlic sausage (appetite) and chapel incense (the Church), closed with a three-word joke.
- Cruelty is a gesture, the boot tap "almost companionable".
- The closing proverb is coined on the spot out of the gaoler's own word, and it is short enough to shout at a recruit.

Steal: judgement delivered as a smell or a gesture; one home-made proverb per scene, not three.

### T2. "I counted, as I always had" (ch01 L91-95, 87 words)

> When they dragged me back to the cellar and chained me in my place against the wall, my hands were raw meat and my shoulders throbbed with each breath. I closed my eyes, not in prayer, but in calculation.
>
> How many guards at the door. How often they changed shifts. Which men wore keys at their belts and which only carried cudgels. Where the buckets were stored. How many steps from the stair to the courtyard above.
>
> I counted, as I always had. Horses, men, paces, opportunities.

Why it is alive:
- This is the engine of the voice. Counting is his fear response and his professional tool at once.
- The verbless sentences are allowed because they are an inventory: things a prisoner can count. Set them beside the abstract triad at ch07 L163 ("Wariness. Interest. The calculation of a creature deciding whether to trust."), which counts nothing.
- "not in prayer, but in calculation" is the one kind of "not X, but Y" that earns its place, because Y is paid out at once in five concrete questions.

Steal: when he is frightened, give him something to count.

### T3. "Horses would hate this" (ch02 L59-61, 60 words)

> We were herded up a gangplank slick with sea and tar. The ship towered over us, the smell of old voyages clinging to her planks. As my bare feet touched the wood, the planks flexed under my weight, shifting subtly as the water lifted and dropped the hull. For a moment an old, absurd thought surfaced.
>
> Horses would hate this.

Why it is alive:
- Chained, branded and bound for a plantation, his first professional judgement is about footing.
- The joke arrives exactly where the fear should, and nobody explains it.
- Bare feet, flexing planks, sea and tar: the body reads the ship before the mind does.

Steal: the absurd practical thought in the place where a weaker book puts the dramatic feeling.

### T4. "Kanka would have nudged my shoulder" (ch06 L31, 51 words)

> They took our horses. As I dismounted, the coastal animal tossed its head and snorted. I patted its neck without thinking, feeling the warm hide, smelling sweat and salt. My bandaged fingers ached with the remembered absence of Kanka. This horse did not know me. Kanka would have nudged my shoulder.

Why it is alive:
- Grief arrives through a horse that is not his. The last sentence works by negative space: what this horse does not do.
- The order is an act (patted), the senses (hide, sweat, salt), the body (fingers ached), then two flat facts.

Soft spot: "the remembered absence of Kanka" is the only abstract phrase, and the last two sentences already do its work. "My bandaged fingers ached." is enough.

### T5. "The ground lies to you" (ch07 L29-31, 73 words)

> “We did not ride from Madurai to school our mounts like children on their first pony,” he said. “My men ride to break lines, not to dance with crabs.”
>
> “Your men ride on hard earth,” I said. “Here the ground lies to you. It looks firm, but it drinks the hoof. If you charge a Dutch square and your horse stumbles on the turn, you are not a soldier, you are a target.”

Why it is alive:
- Expert knowledge in plain words. The metaphor lives in a verb from his trade: the ground "drinks the hoof".
- The argument ends on a consequence, not a principle: "you are a target".
- Raza Khan answers in his own idiom ("dance with crabs"). Professionals disagree in their own words.

Steal: verbs from horse work (drink, give, sink, hold, find the bottom) in place of adjectives.

### T6. "The foreign captain who yelled at horses" (ch08 L5, 85 words)

> It was months of sun and salt. My days were spent on the wet sands, shouting until my throat was raw, trying to make Madurai horses and Maravar ponies understand each other. The fishermen watched us from their boats, laughing when a charger stumbled, nodding when a formation held. I ate their fish curry, spicy enough to make a Deccan man weep for milk, and drank their toddy. I became, in a small way, part of the landscape: the foreign captain who yelled at horses.

Why it is alive:
- Months pass through food, noise and a raw throat, not through reflection.
- "weep for milk" is a Deccan man's comparison. A coastal man would reach for water.
- He sees himself from the fishing boats, and the picture is comic. Nobody in the book is funnier about Nagoji than Nagoji.

Steal: let time pass through meals and chores.

### T7. "The gods have poor handwriting" (ch13 L223-237, 129 words)

> “You ever think you would be here, sahib?” he asked, using the old title out of habit. “At the edge of a southern sea, about to ride at Dutch guns for a king who is not yours?”
>
> “The gods have poor handwriting,” I said. “If they wrote this in my horoscope, the astrologer must have fallen asleep.”
>
> He chuckled, then grew serious.
>
> “The men trust you,” he said. “They grumble about sand and salt, but when you say 'ride', they will.”
>
> “That is what frightens me,” I said.
>
> He looked up, surprised.
>
> “You are not afraid of Dutch guns?” he asked.
>
> “Guns I understand,” I said. “Men who follow because they believe you see further than they do, that is a weight I am still learning how to carry.”

Why it is alive:
- Fate is handled as a clerk's complaint: bad handwriting, a sleepy astrologer.
- Fear is admitted once, sideways, and not about death. "Guns I understand." What frightens him is men who follow.
- Short lines, "sahib" out of old habit, Dhanaji's surprise: two soldiers talking, not a dialogue about themes.

Soft spot: "a weight I am still learning how to carry" is borrowed language. "Guns I understand" carries the passage on its own.

### T8. "A square that turns is no longer a square" (ch14 L133-137, 86 words)

> We hit the Dutch square from the side.
>
> The impact was terrible. Horses screaming, steel ringing, the wet crunch of bodies falling. The Dutch tried to turn, to reform, but a square that turns is no longer a square. It is a crowd.
>
> I drove my horse toward the gap we had torn. Dhanaji was beside me, then ahead of me, his sword already bloody. The Deccan riders poured through, and suddenly we were among them, too close for their muskets, too fast for their bayonets.

Why it is alive:
- Battle from the saddle: impact, noise, then the tactical fact.
- The maxim explains a mechanism. It could go in a drill book.
- "too close for their muskets, too fast for their bayonets" is tactical arithmetic, not a lyrical cadence.
- No slow motion, no "time stretched", no "the world narrowed".

Steal: in combat, state the mechanism and keep moving.

### T9. "The way a lamp runs out of oil" (ch16 L3-9, 148 words)

> The horse died on the third day of the fever.
>
> I had bought him from a Tamil trader, a grey with a black mane, not as fine as the mount I had lost in the storm, but steady, patient, willing to learn the sand. I had named him Megha, for the monsoon clouds he reminded me of.
>
> When he would not rise that morning, I knew. I sat with him in the stable while the grooms kept their distance, watching his flanks heave, feeling his neck grow cold under my hand. He did not struggle at the end. He simply stopped, the way a lamp runs out of oil.
>
> I buried him myself, on the hill behind Padmini Amma's estate, in the shade of a tamarind tree. The ground was hard. It took hours. By the time I finished, my hands were blistered and the sun was setting.

Why it is alive:
- Even in grief he gives provenance: a Tamil trader, a grey with a black mane, a name with its reason.
- The single simile is domestic and exact.
- Grief is labour. "The ground was hard. It took hours." These short sentences are facts about digging, not drumbeats.

Soft spot: "the mount I had lost in the storm" contradicts ch07 L145 and the prequel (`front_matter_prequel.md` L23), where Kanka dies at Vasai. See Appendix D.

### T10. "The third I only broke" (ch17 L31-37, 50 words)

> “My uncle says you once killed three men in Goa with a chain still on your wrist.”
>
> “Your uncle exaggerates,” I said. “It was two men. The third I only broke.”
>
> The boy considered this.
>
> “I would like to learn how to break men,” he said. “When I am older.”

Why it is alive:
- Humour by precision. He corrects the legend downward, and the corrected number is worse.
- The boy's reply turns it cold. Nagoji does not react, and the page does not tell us to shudder.

Steal: correct the legend with a smaller, uglier number.

### T11. "Father says the sky is more unreliable than the Peshwa" (ch19 L25-27, 79 words)

> We heard from a trader that you did not drown. I would have been offended if the sea had kept you. This land needs you more than salt does.
>
> Our parents live. Their backs bend more than before, but they still walk the fields at dawn and argue with each other over how much seed to risk in each plot. Father says the sky is more unreliable than the Peshwa. I am not sure which is the greater insult.

Why it belongs here, although it is his brother's letter and not his narration:
- This is the family voice he grew up in, and it explains his. Farm-dry, weather first, politics measured against rain.
- "I would have been offended if the sea had kept you" is love disguised as a complaint.

Steal: affection as grievance; Deccan farm references (seed, dawn fields, a sky that fails you).

### T12. "Only his eyes were wrong" (ch22 L71-75, 63 words)

> Only his eyes were wrong.
>
> They were too still.
>
> Devout men have a certain softness around the gaze, a readiness to be distracted by a bird or a child or a stray dog. This man's eyes moved once, to take in the courtyard, and then fixed on the heir with the unblinking focus of a man who has burned away every other thought.

Why it is alive:
- A sentry's knowledge of crowds: devout men are distractible "by a bird or a child or a stray dog". Years of watching are packed into one list.
- Danger is read from the absence of ordinary behaviour, which is how a guard actually reads it.
- The short lines are observations, not punches.

Soft spot: "burned away every other thought" leans lurid. The stillness already says it.

### T13. "The water in the basin turned pink" (ch23 L383-385, 45 words)

> I saluted and left, feeling smaller than I had in years.
>
> That night I scrubbed the valley off my skin until my knuckles stung. The water in the basin turned pink, then clear, and still I could taste smoke at the back of my throat.

Why it is alive:
- Guilt as a chore: scrubbing, stinging knuckles, a basin changing colour, a taste that stays.
- One plainly named feeling ("smaller than I had in years"), and then the body takes over.

Steal: aftermath in water and skin. The last page of the book (ch28 L301) says "Some things do not wash." This passage showed it, in the right register, five chapters earlier.

### T14. "He was very particular about his cuffs" (ch24 L223-227, 105 words)

> “In Goa,” I said, “there was an officer. He would come to the cell each morning in a coat like the one the king showed us. White, with red beneath. Brass buttons. He would check his cuffs before he gave orders. He was very particular about his cuffs.”
>
> Lannoy said nothing.
>
> “The coat was always clean,” I continued. “Even when there was blood on the floor. He would step around it carefully so as not to stain his shoes. Then he would leave, and the guards would do what he had ordered, and the coat would walk out as spotless as it had walked in.”

Why it is alive:
- Horror remembered through one fastidious habit. No "evil", no "haunted", no "trauma".
- The repeated "particular about his cuffs" is testimony, a man going back over a detail, not a rhythm device.
- Lannoy says nothing, and the book lets the silence stand.

Steal: let an enemy's small habit carry the moral weight.

### T15. "Like a loaded musket" (ch28 L263, 61 words)

> I thought of one night on her western verandah, long before Savitri's banners rose, when I almost told her I would lay down my marches if she asked. The words sat on my tongue like a loaded musket. She turned away to scold a niece for running on wet stone, and I swallowed them. Perhaps that mercy was hers, not mine.

Why it is alive:
- Love as a thing almost said. The simile comes from his world, and it is primed and dangerous.
- Household life interrupts: a niece running on wet stone.
- He ends in doubt, not revelation: "Perhaps that mercy was hers, not mine."

Steal: this is the late-book register at its best. Make it the model for every Revathi scene.

## 3. Anatomy of the voice

### 3.1 The concrete vocabulary of his world

His nouns come from six stores. Reach into these before reaching for an abstraction.

Two habits matter more than any single word.

- **Prices and estimates.** A charger costs "fifty pagodas if you bought it young" (ch07 L151). A voyage takes "three days. Four if they are unlucky. Longer if someone whispers wrong directions into the ear of a pilot." (ch06 L101). An estimate hedged like a scout's report is pure Nagoji.
- **Work verbs.** The ground "drinks the hoof" (ch07 L31). A ring bolt tears out with "a sound like a tooth being pulled from rotten gum" (ch03 L99). His hands have "tied more saddle girths than ribs" (ch16 L43).

For the words that are not his (percent, yards, pounds, half past eleven, shockwave, thermal, logistics, asset, timeline, machine, berserkers), see Appendix C.

### 3.2 Where his similes and metaphors come from

His images come from the same six stores.

- **Horses and horse dealing.** The Marakkar captain looks him over "the way a horse trader's eyes move over new stock" (ch04 L129). He learns Malayalam "Like a horse finding its footing on new ground" (ch09 L80). "Muskets are like horses. They draw every eye in a space." (ch03 L31).
- **Deccan weather and land.** Dripping water "steady as monsoon rain on a Deccan roof" (ch01 L9). Kanka's "temper like monsoon lightning" (ch07 L145).
- **Kitchen and household.** A sky "the colour of stale rice water" (ch04 L21). A horse dying "the way a lamp runs out of oil" (ch16 L7). Padmini's "balancing pots on a shelf" (ch25 L326), which he answers in kind.
- **Forts and weapons.** Deccan forts "rose from crags like stacked fists" (ch16 L187). Unspoken words "like a loaded musket" (ch28 L263).
- **Money and ledgers.** Ramayyan's gaze "like a clerk’s scale, weighing gold against brass" (ch08 L25). Choosing to stay because he found "fewer deaths in this column than in the others" (ch16 L329).

Images imported from outside his life fail every time, and they are easy to spot: "two hawks who had spotted the same thermal" (ch20 L17); "like dancers locked in an awkward embrace" (ch22 L153); "droplets clung to her skin like scattered pearls" (ch17 L207); a floor that "gleamed like a dark mirror" (ch17 L49); "like being born through a tunnel of stone" (ch02 L25); "He did not run; he flowed, like water finding a crack in a dam." (ch23 L425); gunners "like priests around an altar, their faith not in any god but in the mathematics of fire" (ch24 L681); the sea "like the hand of an angry god" (ch03 L231).

One pair shows the difference better than any rule:

- Passes: "peace here was just a word for the time it took to reload" (ch10 L187). A soldier's joke with a musket in it.
- Fails: "Peace is a fragile skin on a rotting fruit." (ch23 L415). The fruit comes from nowhere in his life, and the line would sit happily on a calendar.

The test: if the second half of the comparison is something he has ridden, eaten, priced, loaded, dug or survived, keep it. If it came from a library, cut it. And keep them rare. The first edition has 80 uses of "as if" and 84 of "like a". His best images land because there are few of them.

### 3.3 His humour

Eight mechanisms, all of them small.

1. **The shrinking verb.** He reduces horror to a small verb. Asked what the Portuguese did to him: "Asked questions" (ch04 L107). Asked if they questioned him: "They tried." (ch06 L77). Of the shipwreck: "The storm did not like any of us" (ch05 L9).
2. **The corrected number.** "It was two men. The third I only broke." (ch17 L33).
3. **The literal reply.** The king says his riders move like fish, not bulls. Nagoji: "Fish do not charge muskets" (ch07 L113), then concedes the point.
4. **The practical priority at the wrong moment.** "Horses would hate this." (ch02 L61).
5. **The coined proverb.** "Stubborn is better than broken in any language." (ch01 L23). “All ships break,” I said. “The question is when and how.” (ch02 L115).
6. **Theology as a trade dispute.** God "seems to favour whoever has the better powder" (ch01 L73). “Gods and I have an understanding,” I said. “I try not to waste their time.” (ch10 L27). "The gods have poor handwriting" (ch13 L225).
7. **Self-portrait from outside.** "the foreign captain who yelled at horses" (ch08 L5). "A Maratha who has tied more saddle girths than ribs" (ch16 L43). "I am less sure about their rider." (ch22 L399).
8. **Gallows economy.** Told his horse was shot: "Then he died better than I will" (ch01 L57).

The rules behind them: he never laughs at his own line, never explains it, and never adds a reaction shot ("I smiled wryly"). His joke is usually shorter than the line it answers. He does not joke down at servants, fishermen or the beaten. He is drier than the people around him. Padmini, Ibrahim and the king are openly witty (see 3.9), and Nagoji is the straight man more often than the wit.

### 3.4 Sentence habits and rhythm

- **Default sentence.** Subject, a verb of work, a concrete object, past tense. Adjectives are physical (slick, raw, lathered, ridged) and rarely doubled.
- **Long sentences are inventories.** Of things seen (ch01 L17), of prices (ch07 L151), of smells ("fish, coconut oil, the faint sharpness of toddy", ch04 L25). A long Nagoji sentence has commas full of nouns, not clauses full of ideas.
- **Short sentences carry facts and actions.** "The ground was hard. It took hours." (ch16 L9). They are not verdicts ("Every choice binds." ch25 L338) and not one-word reveals ("Anticipation." ch07 L139).
- **Estimates come hedged,** the way a scout reports (ch06 L101).
- **In dialogue his answers are shorter than the questions.** "You raided them?" "Yes." "You know their forts?" "Some." (ch05 L147-153); the clipped answers to the king in ch06 L59-77. He parries; he does not orate. The exceptions are his two audition speeches (ch05 L161; ch06 L149-153), and they belong: those are the only times he is selling himself.
- **Watching is sometimes the action.** He is a watcher, so "I watched" can be the event itself: "Watched which chiefs spoke and which stayed silent." (ch11 L307). Keep it when watching is his job in the scene. Cut it when it is only a camera move.
- **Paragraphs end on a fact, a gesture or a line of speech.** When a paragraph ends on a summary ("This was where the Portuguese forged information." ch01 L45), that sentence is nearly always the one to cut.
- **Retrospect is allowed, sparingly.** "Kayal would carry me through Colachel and beyond" (ch07 L183) is a fair memoir move. Once a chapter is plenty.

### 3.5 Emotion and fear

The best passages share one pattern:

1. **One beat in the body.** "My throat closed." (ch01 L55). "The back of my neck prickled" (ch06 L45). "I felt the hairs on my arms rise." (ch22 L77).
2. **Then concrete things.** A memory in nouns (Kanka's mane on winter mornings, his stride, musket fire near Chaul), or whatever is in front of him.
3. **Then an act or a flat line of speech.** "I forced the memory down." "Then he died better than I will."

Feeling travels through four channels:

- **Horses.** What he cannot say about Keshavrao or his parents, he can say about Kanka, Megha and Kayal (T4, T9).
- **Soldier's custom.** Grief deferred by camp law: "We would mourn later, in camp, with liquor and song and stories." Then: "This was my field now." (ch03 L215-217).
- **Chores.** Digging a grave (T9), scrubbing in a basin (T13), a bandage undone and wound "tighter and neater" (ch16 L45).
- **Counting.** Fear becomes an inventory (T2). Losing count is how the page should show him losing himself (see C6).

He is plainly afraid of the sea, of iron, and of men who trust him (T7). He admits it late and small. Pain he notices after the fact: "Only then did I notice the sting in my palm" (ch22 L257).

Love follows the same rules: practical care (ch16 L45), jokes (the cowrie shells, ch20 L159-161), words not said (T15).

Where the first edition breaks the pattern, it adds a second beat that names or explains what the first beat showed: "a strange mixture tightened in my chest" (ch07 L135); "I felt something like pride" (ch16 L435); "I understood then" (ch17 L183, ch28 L67); "For the first time, I was home." (ch20 L408); "my voice thick" (ch17 L141). Cut the second beat.

### 3.6 How he sees other people

The general rule: every people is seen first through its tools, food, work and habits. Judgement comes later, and it is earned by something he watched them do.

**The Portuguese.** A regime of paper and liturgy. His name becomes "a number the clerk scratched" (ch01 L7). Prisoners are woken "under cover of bells" (ch02 L3), and João hums the same hymn while he sorts them (ch02 L9). A sailor prices the hold in cruzados "as if we were sacks of pepper" (ch02 L107). He is exact about individuals: Duarte's ink-stained fingers and the calluses that say he once held "something heavier than a quill" (ch01 L15), the officer's cuffs (T14). He files hesitation as intelligence: "Men who hesitate are men who can be used." (ch02 L39). He does not call them devils or evil in narration. When "white devils" appears, it is Ponnan, angry (ch23 L317).

**The Dutch.** At a distance they are drill and ships. The guard at the watch tower says their captain "drills them until they piss in step" (ch11 L51), and Nagoji admits "until I see him on land, it is hard not to think of him as part of the ship" (ch11 L381). He respects their discipline as a professional: the captain shows "the same quality I had seen in Deccan officers who lived for drill and order" (ch11 L375). His contempt is for the Company, not its men: Ramayyan's "They serve a company" (ch14 L75), and the unpaid Germans, Danes and Frenchmen of ch16 L169-171. Lannoy becomes a colleague before he is a friend: "not because we liked each other, but because Ramayyan liked the results" (ch16 L201).

**Nairs and the matrilineal houses.** Seen against his own village. At home the women "moved like shadows at the edge"; here "they flowed through the centre" (ch08 L117). He reports his own caste reflex without apology ("We do not plough." ch08 L33) and his slowness honestly (ch09 L80). He notices economics before romance: pepper sorted on mats, grain in the granary, who pays for the lamp oil (ch08 L57, ch10 L35-53). Nair soldiers are young men with talismans; "Others joked too loudly, voices a shade too high." (ch13 L73). A recruit's verdict on Dutch ships, "houses turned on their sides" with "too many sticks" (ch13 L81), is the kind of line only a local could give him.

**The Travancore court.** Defined against Pune: no carpets, no high hall (ch06 L5); a king on a low platform, spear against his shoulder, one cloth over a bare chest (ch06 L37-39). "Court in Travancore did not look like court in Pune." (ch11 L171). Power shows in small movements: fingers on a spear shaft (ch06 L93), a jaw tightening (ch11 L147), eyes "the kind that measure before they strike" (ch06 L53). Ramayyan is judged by the standard Nagoji holds himself to: "A general who cannot count is a butcher. Ramayyan could count, and he could kill." (ch14 L167).

**Marakkars, Mukkuvar, Syrian Christians, the Jews of Kochi.** Seen through the sea and trade, and allowed their own images: Antony's "anchors act like tethers for goats" (ch14 L59); Ibrahim's "The sea does not care about flags" (ch11 L407); Thoma's "The metal does not care which king pays for the mould." (ch24 L659). The risk in the late chapters is that these men start giving history lectures (ch24 L713-767). Keep them to their trades.

### 3.7 Marathi, Malayalam, Portuguese and the rest

What the book does well:

- **Partial comprehension.** He hears Malayalam in pieces: "I caught only fragments. Eight houses. Vow. Chaver. Heir." (ch22 L87). He guesses at the word *kapitan* and is not sure (ch04 L99). The translation scenes in ch06 turn the language gap into drama. This is the most honest thing in the book about being a foreigner, and it should be the default.
- **Konkani as the bridge.** Fishermen, Ibrahim and Ramayyan reach him through a trade Konkani "close enough to my Marathi" (ch04 L29). Keep that logic. A new Malayalam speaker needs a bridge, or Nagoji needs to miss things.
- **Portuguese as the torture room's language.** "Este", then the English (ch01 L21). He understands more than he admits (ch01 L31-33).
- **Address words that carry a relationship.** sahib (Dhanaji, Keshavrao), anna (his brother), bhau (the fishermen), Amma and Valiyamma, Ayya (Antony to him, him to the temple priest), Diwanji (Ibrahim to Ramayyan).

What goes wrong:

- **Dictionary glosses.** Padmini gives the Sanskrit etymology of *sambandham* ("From the Sanskrit", ch08 L151). A candi is converted into English pounds (ch10 L57). Nobody in 1740 explained a word to a soldier that way.
- **Guidebook passages.** The *nalukettu* floor "polished with egg whites and coconut oil" (ch17 L49). The wedding feast with its menu of italicised dishes and a recipe (ch20 L280-302), including the claim that sambar was "invented by your King Sambhaji" (ch20 L300), which a Maratha would be the first to doubt.
- **Inconsistent forms.** *kapitan* (ch04, ch05, ch18) against Kappittan (ch15, ch16, ch24); Valiya Kappittan (ch15 L223) against Valia Karyakkar (ch18 L23); Ponnam (ch07 L17, L25) against Ponnan (ch07 L37 onward); De Lannoy against Lannoy, sometimes in one scene; diwan (ch06 to ch10) then Dalawa (ch13 onward); chaver in roman (ch22) and in italics (ch23); *huzurat* in lower case at the start of a sentence (ch05 L145).

The rule of thumb: **he glosses what is foreign to him and never what is native to him.** He would not explain bhakri, sardar, chauth or talwar; this is his account, and context carries them. He would explain tharavadu, kalari and chumkam, because he had to learn them, and the gloss should arrive as the moment he learned: who told him, what it cost, what he got wrong. For the style sheet (the author's call): italicise a non-English word on its first use in each chapter and set it in roman after that; fix one spelling per name in `glossary.md`; if the diwan to Dalawa change stays, make it a moment where he learns the proper title.

### 3.8 What he never does

1. Narrate his own growth. No "I understood then" (ch17 L183, ch28 L67), no "Travancore was changing." / "So was I." (ch16 L429-431).
2. Name a feeling and then explain it (ch07 L135-139).
3. Explain what someone else meant after showing it (ch01 L55; ch11 L265, "He had wanted to see who flinched.").
4. Moralise at the end of a chapter. Twenty-three of the twenty-eight chapters currently end on a summary, a maxim or a motif line (Appendix A).
5. Coin maxims about life. His maxims are about horses, powder, ground and men under orders.
6. Turn weather or ground into philosophy: "If storms had taught me anything" (ch09 L216); a burned strip that "did not care about our hearts" (ch26 L257).
7. Use library images: mirrors, pearls, dancers, thermals, priests at altars (3.2).
8. Use modern units, clock times or office words (Appendix C).
9. Call enemies evil or devils in narration. He describes the tool and the habit.
10. Understand every language in the room.
11. Praise himself. Others' praise is reported, and he undercuts it: "I am only the voice today." (ch07 L123).
12. Describe a place as a guidebook would. Places are described by use: what a horse can do on the ground, what a gun can see from a wall, what a room is for.
13. Pray for abstractions. "I prayed for the focus of the cobra, the patience of the stone." (ch13 L281) belongs to someone else. The Nagoji of ch01 L73 leaves God "to the Brahmins".
14. Repeat his own best lines. A good line said twice becomes a tic (Appendix B).

## 4. Contrast: eight passages that sound machine-made

Each is quoted exactly, then diagnosed against the real voice, then rewritten in one line. The rewrites are models of register, not final copy; the author decides the words.

### C1. Explaining the subtext (ch01 L55)

> My throat closed. Kanka's black mane against my cheek on winter mornings, his easy stride, the way he had carried me through musket fire near Chaul. I forced the memory down. They wanted to see grief; they wanted to use it as a lever.

How it differs: the first three sentences are the voice at full strength: one beat in the body, three remembered things in nouns, suppression as an act. The fourth steps out of the cell to brief the reader on interrogation technique. Nagoji has been questioned for days. A man who already knows the game does not explain it; he plays it, and his next line ("Then he died better than I will") is the play. The matched semicolon halves ("They wanted... they wanted") are a machine rhythm; he does not build sentences in pairs when one would do.

Rewrite: cut the last sentence, or hand the reader the lever as an object: *João had the tongs in his hand and his eyes on my face.*

### C2. The emotion inventory (ch07 L135-139)

> As I rode back down toward the strip of wet sand where Madurai and Maravar horsemen waited, catching their breath, a strange mixture tightened in my chest.
>
> Fear, yes. Responsibility, sharp as any spear point. But also something I had not felt since before Goa, before the dungeon, before the storm.
>
> Anticipation.

How it differs: a "strange mixture" unpacked into three named feelings in abstract nouns (fear, responsibility, anticipation); a borrowed simile attached to an abstraction ("sharp as any spear point"); a triple "before" for cadence; a one-word paragraph as the reveal; and "something" standing in for the thing itself. The real voice gives a feeling one physical beat and then a job. In T4 his grief for Kanka is one ache and one thing a strange horse fails to do.

Rewrite: *Halfway down the slope I caught myself already sorting the Madurai horses in my head, the ones that had kept their feet from the ones that would get a man killed.*

### C3. Love as abstraction (ch16 L121-123)

> What happened next was not the coupling of poems, all moonlight and whispered endearments. It was something rawer, two people who had both learned that the world takes without asking, choosing for once to take something for themselves.
>
> She was careful of my ribs. I was careful of the scars she carried that I could not see. We did not speak of love. We did not need to. The word would have been too small for what moved between us, grief and want and the desperate hunger to feel something other than the weight of the dead.

How it differs: "not the coupling of poems... It was something rawer" is the "Not X. Y." formula with "something" in the middle. "the world takes without asking" and "too small for what moved between us, grief and want and the desperate hunger" are generalities no soldier writes about his own night. The one live sentence ("She was careful of my ribs.") is undone at once by its abstract twin ("the scars she carried that I could not see"). In the real voice intimacy is care of the body, written the way he writes about horses, and the book set it up earlier in the same chapter, when Revathi rewound his bandage "tighter and neater" (ch16 L45).

Rewrite: *She was careful of my ribs, and afterwards she found the end of the bandage in the dark and tied it again, tighter than I had.*

### C4. The moral ending (ch17 L277-281)

> She left. The lamp guttered and died. I lay in the darkness of my own cellar, in my own house, and felt for the first time that the walls around me were not a cage but a foundation.
>
> Some burdens are not chosen. Some names are not earned. Some debts are paid by becoming what is needed, and learning to call it home.
>
> And some doors, once opened, are never fully closed again.

How it differs: "felt for the first time" (a style-sheet kill phrase); "not a cage but a foundation" (the correction formula, with two metaphors he has never handled); then three "Some..." maxims and a fourth about doors. The chapter closes on a sermon about names and debts instead of the object in front of him. The true ending is already on the page, "The lamp guttered and died.", and the chapter has given the cellar a smell: "the smell of old rice" (ch17 L71).

Rewrite: *She left. The lamp guttered and died, and I lay in the smell of old rice until the first servant began sweeping the granary floor over my head.*

### C5. The litany and the question (ch19 L181-183)

> These hands had held Maratha reins on Deccan dust. They had gripped Portuguese chains in Goa's darkness. They had pulled Keshavrao toward a rope that broke. They had trained Travancore cavalry in wet sand and held a dying chaver's wrist while a king drove steel through his throat.
>
> Whose hands were they now?

How it differs: the paragraph before it is superb, nails grown back "wrong, ridged and discoloured" (ch19 L179). Then the voice leaves the hands to recite the plot: four "had" clauses ("These hands had held... They had gripped...") about events the reader lived through, closed by a rhetorical question. He uses his hands. He does not hold a service over them.

Rewrite: *I closed the left hand as if on Kanka's reins; the thumb the Portuguese had worked on still would not lie flat.* (The thumb is ch01 L81.)

### C6. Violence as mood (ch23 L295)

> The world narrowed. Heat, screams, powder smoke, fresh blood, all of it sang to something dark in me. I was not just fighting. I was hunting.

How it differs: "The world narrowed" is stock. A list of real things (heat, screams, smoke, blood) is thrown away on "sang to something dark in me", a vague depth phrase with a musical metaphor attached to nothing. "I was not just fighting. I was hunting." is the correction formula. It names the bloodlust instead of showing the one sign that would frighten Nagoji about himself, because the book has taught us that his mind is a tally (T2).

Rewrite: *After the third man I stopped counting them, and I had counted everything since I was a boy in a fort courtyard.*

### C7. Epigram dialogue (ch24 L579-583 and L635-641)

> “You do not speak to me like the others do,” he said.
>
> “Should I?”
>
> “No,” he said. “The others speak around the truth. You speak through it.”

Then, at L635-641:

> "Thank you, Pillai," he said. "For not treating me like glass."
>
> “Glass breaks,” I said. “You are not glass. You are iron that has not yet been forged. The fire is uncomfortable. But it is necessary.”
>
> He smiled, that wry, Revathi-like smile again.
>
> “Then I will try to enjoy the burning,” he said.

How it differs: a fourteen-year-old and a cavalry officer trade a finished chiasmus ("around the truth... through it"), then build one extended metaphor between them (glass, iron, forge, fire, burning), each completing the other's figure. This is machine dialogue: everyone speaks in quotations, and they agree on the imagery. Nagoji teaches in commands with a joke folded in: "The gun does not fear your heartbeat. Make it go slower than the drum." (ch24 L73); "Now do it when someone is shouting in your ear" (ch24 L79).

Rewrite (his reply at L637): *"Glass would not have come back the next morning. Keep your guard hand higher."*

### C8. The last page (ch28 L289-305)

> And here, on these walls, I stood guard over the thing we had built from the ashes of what they had lost.
>
> That was the price.
>
> That was always the price.
>
> The monsoon came that year, as it always did. The rains fell on the forts and the fields and the roads. They fell on Kochi, where Savitri's garden drank them gratefully. They fell on Velinadu, where Revathi sat and remembered and my grandchildren ran laughing through wet corridors.
>
> They fell on me, on these walls, on this kingdom that had eaten so many smaller ones to become something that might survive.
>
> I lifted my face to the rain and let it wash away what it could.
>
> Some things do not wash.
>
> Some things we carry.
>
> For as long as we live, and in the stories that outlast us.

How it differs: the page has its image (rain on a wall) and buries it under chant: "That was the price." twice; a four-part "They fell on..." run; then three closing maxims ending on "the stories that outlast us", which Savitri has already said ("And stories outlast walls.", ch27 L219). "Some things do not wash." states what T13 showed in a basin. An old cavalryman ends on a wall, doing what he has always done.

Rewrite: *I lifted my face to the rain and stayed on the wall until the watch changed, counting the lamps as they were lit along the ramparts, as I always had.* (The last four words answer ch01 L95.)

## 6. Rules of the voice

1. **Touch before meaning.** Every paragraph holds at least one thing he could touch, count, ride, eat, price or smell. When a sentence rests on an abstract noun (weight, truth, illusion, something, destiny, foundation), replace the noun with the object that carries it, or cut the sentence.
2. **One beat in the body, then an act.** A feeling gets one physical sign (throat, neck, hands, stomach), and then he does or says something. No inventories of feelings, no "something shifted", no "for the first time I felt". At most one plainly named feeling per scene, in a short sentence (T13).
3. **Never explain what was just shown.** After a look, a joke, a gesture or a silence, delete the sentence that says what it meant (C1).
4. **Give him something to count.** Men, horses, paces, days, coins. In a frightening scene counting is his voice; when he loses count, the reader knows he has lost himself (T2, C6).
5. **Images from his six stores only.** Horse and tack; arms and forts; money and ledgers; the Deccan farm and its weather; the coast he learned; prison and ship. If the second half of a simile is not something he has ridden, eaten, priced, loaded, dug or survived, cut it. One simile a scene is generous (3.2).
6. **The drill-ground test for maxims.** Keep a maxim only if he could say it to a recruit and it would change what the recruit did ("a square that turns is no longer a square"). Cut maxims about life, love, choice, stories, history or the sea.
7. **Humour makes things smaller.** Understatement, the corrected number, the literal reply, the practical thought at the wrong moment. He never explains a joke, never laughs at his own line, and never jokes down at the weak (3.3).
8. **Foreigners by tools and habits.** Portuguese by books, bells, tongs and cuffs. Dutch by drill, spyglasses, coats and ledgers. Nairs by kalari, granary and household. The court by mats, spears and silences. Moral judgement comes from what he watched them do; no "evil" and no "devils" in his narration.
9. **He hears languages as a foreigner.** Malayalam in fragments, Konkani as the bridge, Portuguese from the torture room. Gloss what is foreign to him by how he learned it, never by etymology, and never gloss Marathi. Italicise on first use in each chapter; one spelling per name, fixed in `glossary.md` (3.7).
10. **Period words only.** Paces, not yards. Hands for a horse's height. Pagodas, chuckrams and candis, not pounds or percent. A day's ride, not a clock time. No logistics, asset, timeline, machine, mechanical, shockwave, thermal, efficiency or berserker (Appendix C).
11. **Plain syntax; fragments only for inventories.** Subject, verb of work, concrete object. No "It was not X. It was Y." reveals, no one-word paragraphs, no triads of abstract fragments. Verbless sentences are allowed when they list things he could count; those are inventories, and they do not count against the style sheet's limit of three fragments for effect.
12. **Places by use, not by brochure.** Ground is described by what a horse can do on it, walls by what a gun can see from them, rooms by who eats and sleeps there. No architecture lectures, no menus.
13. **Other people speak their own trade.** Padmini in grain and threats, Ibrahim in sea and trade, Ramayyan in accounts, the king in short orders, Lannoy in stone and pay, Revathi in land and lineage. Apply the swap test, and allow each character one epigram per scene at most (3.9).
14. **End on a thing.** The last line of a scene or chapter is an act, an object or a line of speech. Never a moral, a "Some X..." chain, "For now", "Together", or a restatement of the chapter's theme. Look one to three lines up: the true ending is usually already there (Appendix A).
15. **Say a good line once.** Each motif gets one home. A striking line repeated in another chapter becomes a tic (Appendix B).

### 6b. Protect these, so the edit does not flatten him

The plan's main risk is over-cutting. These look like tics on a scan but belong to the voice:

- His coined proverbs that pass the drill-ground test (T1, T8; ch02 L115).
- "Not X, but Y" when Y is paid out at once in concrete detail (ch01 L91).
- Verbless inventories that are genuine counts: "Pliers. Hammers. Wooden wedges." (ch01 L43), the questions at ch01 L93.
- Memoir retrospect ("Years later", "Kayal would carry me"), once a chapter.
- His two audition speeches (ch05 L161; ch06 L149-153), the only times he orates, because he is auditioning for his life.
- The wit of Padmini, Ibrahim and the king. Cut their oracle lines, not their jokes.
- Hedged estimates and prices, even when they slow a scene. They are how he thinks.

## Appendix D. Continuity snags noticed while reading

Not an audit, only what surfaced during the voice read. Each affects a line the edit will touch.

1. **Kanka.** A black mare killed at Vasai by a Portuguese ball (`front_matter_prequel.md` L23; ch07 L145). But ch01 L55 gives "his easy stride", ch01 L53 has João say "We shot it when we brought you in", and ch16 L5 speaks of "the mount I had lost in the storm".
2. **Kayal at Colachel.** Kayal is a mare who "would carry me through Colachel" (ch07 L183), but at Colachel "My horse reared, screaming. I fought to control him" (ch14 L101).
3. **Ponnan.** Introduced as Ponnam Pandya Deven (ch07 L17, L25), then Ponnan from ch07 L37 onward.
4. **Dhanaji's arrival.** He "survived Goa's dungeons alongside me" (ch13 L221) and says "Four monsoons since we washed up on this coast" (ch16 L21), but chapters 1 to 4 have Nagoji wash up alone, and Dhanaji first appears at the Van Imhoff meeting (ch11 L123) without an arrival.
5. **Why Nagoji left.** "I had fled the Peshwa's justice" (ch12 L145) and "whispers even in my exile" (ch12 L71) contradict his capture in the field (ch01).
6. **De Lannoy's birthplace.** Arras (ch15 L291; ch16 L345) against "In Zeeland, where I was born" (ch24 L239).
7. **Dates.** Ch01 sets the capture in 1738; the prequel puts Nagoji at the fall of Vasai, which was in 1739.
8. **Chapter titles against file names.** Ch14's heading is "The Siege of Colachel" (file: `charge_at_colachel`); ch22's is "Command of the Kayamkulam Frontier" (file: `command_of_the_marches`).
9. **Near-duplicate attacks on the heir.** A chaver at a hill shrine (ch22) and a chaver at Padmanabhaswamy (ch23 L417-449), with the heir frozen both times, plus a festival knife recalled at ch21 L193. Ch24 L491 and L553-563 refer back to a single temple attack. Worth one decision before the line edit reaches Book 4.
