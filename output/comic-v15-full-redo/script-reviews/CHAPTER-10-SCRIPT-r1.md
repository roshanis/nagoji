## Chapter 10: Lessons in Travancore

## PAGE 1

Five panels. Deccan stone against Travancore green. Ramayyan collects him, and the lessons begin at a temple door.

**1.1** Wide, full page width. Memory, in dry ochre tones unlike anything else in the chapter: a Deccan hill fort on black rock, a thick bastion, cannon ports, lime-washed walls under a hard sun. No figure close enough to identify.

> CAPTION: In the Deccan, a kingdom revealed itself in its forts.

> CAPTION: You could stand on a bastion and read a ruler's mind in stone and mortar.

**1.2** Morning in Travancore, full wet green. The door of Nagoji's quarters on Padmini Amma's estate: laterite walls, a tiled roof still dripping from the night's rain, coconut palms. Nagoji (commander sheet: rust-red turban, cream tunic, broad red sash, cream trousers, boots, talwar) stepping out, still settling his sash. Ramayyan already waiting on the path: thin, sharp fine features, receding hair, plain white cloth without a speck of dust, a bundle of palm leaves under one arm.

> CAPTION: In Travancore, I learned, you had to listen in different places.

**1.3** Two-shot, medium. Ramayyan has already turned to go. Nagoji one step behind, following.

> RAMAYYAN: Walk with me, Sawant.

> CAPTION: When the diwan of a kingdom tells you to walk, you do not ask where.

**1.4** Wide. A small temple on a hill above a cluster of houses and pepper gardens. A modest gopuram. Stone steps worn hollow in the centre. Ramayyan and Nagoji climbing; villagers, men and women, stepping aside for Ramayyan, and he gives each one the same small nod.

> CAPTION: A temple to Ayyappa, the forest warrior. Patron of all who carried arms in these hills.

> CAPTION: Every greeting, Ramayyan put away somewhere behind his eyes.

**1.5** The outer courtyard. Oil lamps, bells on chains, incense smoke winding round stone pillars, Brahmins at their rituals in the background (the officiating priest may use the V13 temple-priest sheet). At the innermost threshold Ramayyan has stopped and turned back to face Nagoji.

> RAMAYYAN: You do not have to go closer. The god knows you have your own ways.

> NAGOJI: Gods and I have an understanding. I try not to waste their time.

## PAGE 2

Five panels. The temple as a ledger. Keep the two men still; let the lamps do the moving.

**2.1** In the shade of the mandapa. Both men side by side, the sanctum's lamp glow ahead of them, the bright courtyard behind. The faintest smile on Ramayyan.

> RAMAYYAN: Watch instead.

> RAMAYYAN: Who pays for the oil in those lamps?

> NAGOJI: The temple. From offerings.

**2.2** Nagoji in close profile, thinking. Three small insets along the top edge, what he is seeing in his head: boys at practice in a kalari, women sorting pepper on mats, a bullock cart groaning under sacks of paddy.

> RAMAYYAN (off): Where do the offerings come from?

> NAGOJI: From the fields. From houses like Padmini Amma's.

**2.3** Ramayyan, turned toward the sanctum, counting on his fingers. Above him, an old dark water stain on the stone of the mandapa roof.

> RAMAYYAN: And who pays when the roof leaks? When the priest demands new ornaments for the god? When the elephant that carries him in procession goes lame?

> NAGOJI: The king. If he is wise.

**2.4** Close on Ramayyan. Lamplight in his eyes.

> RAMAYYAN: If he wants men to believe the god favours his reign.

> RAMAYYAN: A temple is not just stone. It is a ledger. Offerings in. Blessings out.

> RAMAYYAN: The names written on both sides matter.

**2.5** Wide, the largest panel on the page. A courtyard wall set with dozens of small niches, a lamp burning in every one. Below it, worshippers: a fisherman, a bare-shouldered labourer, a woman of a landed house. The two men small at the edge, in shadow.

> RAMAYYAN (off): Each of those lamps is paid for by a house. Padmini's line. Fishermen. Men who own no land but rent their backs to others.

> RAMAYYAN (off): When the king asks for extra tax to pay for muskets and forts, they remember whether he feeds their god.

## PAGE 3

Five panels. The market. The argument is loud; the information is in the eyes.

**3.1** Wide establishing. Awnings of cloth and palm leaves. Traders from the interior haggling over sacks of rice and jars of oil. On a raised platform, an Arab merchant in a flowing white robe sits cross-legged, weighing silver coins on a small brass scale while his servant counts sacks of pepper. Nearby, a Syrian Christian scribe at a low desk, writing, a stack of folded paper bills at his elbow. Marakkar men bargaining over pepper and cardamom, their eyes flicking toward a path that runs down to the water. Ramayyan and Nagoji entering at the edge.

> CAPTION: Silver chuckrams. Pepper by the candi, some five hundred pounds a sack, the measure by which fortunes were made or lost on this coast.

> CAPTION: And hundis, that could turn pepper in Travancore into gold in Muscat without a single coin crossing the sea.

**3.2** Two-shot. Nagoji unimpressed. Ramayyan's eyes already somewhere down the row of stalls.

> RAMAYYAN: Do you see the pattern?

> NAGOJI: I see men arguing over coins. That is the same everywhere.

> RAMAYYAN: Look closer.

**3.3** A stall, a table piled with cloth, pepper sacks stacked behind it. Yusuf, a Marakkar captain (NOT Ibrahim: leaner, no scar, a sea captain's short coat), leaning across the table toward the stallholder.

> CAPTION: Yusuf. Ibrahim's cousin, from the coastal hall.

> YUSUF: If your pepper is worm eaten, my mast will refuse to carry it. The Dutch will not pay for rot.

**3.4** The stallholder: a woman in her late twenties, a baby slung on her hip, arms folded. Immovable.

> WOMAN: My pepper has fewer worms than your honesty.

> WOMAN: Your ships carry news as well as goods. When you bring Dutch coins into our land, remember that the king counts them with his fingers, not yours.

**3.5** Yusuf laughing, but his eyes sliding sideways: to Ramayyan and Nagoji in the street, then further down the market to a pale-faced Dutch factor in a dark merchant's coat and hat (a trader, not a blue-coated soldier). Inset, tight: the scribe's pen at Yusuf's side making one tiny change to a figure.

> RAMAYYAN: The king's tax sits between them.

> RAMAYYAN: Too high, the Marakkar captain goes to another coast. Too low, the palace cannot buy Dutch guns to shoot back at Dutch ships.

## PAGE 4

Five panels. The training ground. One page of noise in a quiet chapter, then Ramayyan's verdict.

**4.1** Walking out of the market onto a firm flat track, the coastal fort rising ahead of them.

> NAGOJI: You walk these places every day?

> RAMAYYAN: As often as I can. A diwan who hears only petitions in the court knows half a kingdom.

> RAMAYYAN: The other half lives in the way people say 'our pepper' and 'the king's pepper,' as if they are the same, or as if they are not.

**4.2** Wide. The training ground on a flat strip inland from the fort. Men in white stand in lines two deep, matchlocks on their shoulders. A stocky older European in a stained brown coat (not red, not Dutch blue), belly soft, eyes wary, walking the front rank. He has turned to hail Ramayyan.

> DRILL MASTER: Ram, my friend! You come to see how well they march?

> RAMAYYAN: I come to see whether they can do more than march.

> CAPTION: Not De Lannoy. Not yet.

**4.3** Wide, the loudest panel in the chapter. The drill master in the foreground, scowling back over his shoulder as he barks the order. Behind him the front rank fires as one: flashes along the line, a white sheet of smoke rolling out across the ground. Ramayyan and Nagoji at the side of the frame.

> DRILL MASTER: One thing at a time. First they learn left from right.

> CAPTION: The smell of saltpetre and burned powder wrapped itself around us.

**4.4** The smoke thinning. Ramayyan, entirely unmoved. Nagoji looking at him sidelong.

> RAMAYYAN: Adequate.

> NAGOJI: Praise indeed.

**4.5** Ramayyan turning to Nagoji as the line reloads behind them.

> RAMAYYAN: Our men must learn this European dance, or they will die confused when the Dutch play their drums.

> RAMAYYAN: But this is only one rhythm. Yours is another. A kingdom survives when these rhythms do not trip each other up.

## PAGE 5

Five panels. The backwaters, and Ibrahim, who brings the warmth.

**5.1** Wide. A maze of narrow channels and broad lagoons, palm fronds and sky reflected in shards on the water. Boats everywhere: small ones poled by one or two men, broad low ones piled with sacks. On a narrow quay in the foreground, Ibrahim Marakkar (shorter than the boatmen but broader, scar from his LEFT ear to the jaw, short coat over a vest with bulging pockets) shouting at a boatman heaving one barrel too many aboard.

> CAPTION: Here the kingdom's veins opened.

> IBRAHIM: Do you want to sit on them? If the boat sinks, the fish will have a feast and the Dutch will laugh.

**5.2** Ibrahim breaking off as he sees them, touching his forehead in greeting.

> IBRAHIM: Diwanji. Sawant. Come to see how your horses eat?

> NAGOJI: Horses do not like water. I admire anyone who rides it as if it were a road.

**5.3** Ibrahim grinning, one arm swept out over the lagoon. On the far bank, small and distant, a European fort.

> IBRAHIM: Water is a road. A road no company can draw a line through.

> IBRAHIM: They can put forts on the banks, but the backwaters go where they wish.

**5.4** Ramayyan resting his hand on one of the barrels. Ibrahim at his shoulder.

> RAMAYYAN: Pepper.

> IBRAHIM: And a little cardamom. Enough to flavour a Dutchman's breath and his profits.

> RAMAYYAN: And for the king?

**5.5** Ibrahim, hand on his heart, mock-wounded. Ramayyan's eyes crinkling at the edges.

> IBRAHIM: His share is already in his godowns.

> IBRAHIM: I am not fool enough to cheat the man whose scribes have longer memories than my own mother.

## PAGE 6

Five panels. The walk back. The day's places become one picture, and Ramayyan names the war that is already on.

**6.1** Leaving the quay in late gold light, the two men on a bund between paddy and water. Ramayyan talks without looking at him.

> RAMAYYAN: Horses carry men to battle. Boats carry the coin that pays for their feed.

> RAMAYYAN: Temples carry the stories that make those men willing to die. Markets carry the arguments over who deserves what.

> RAMAYYAN: If you ride only on the training field, you will know how to move men. If you walk these other paths, you will know why they move.

**6.2** Wide and high. From the climbing path, the whole day's country laid out below in the westering sun: the hill temple, the market awnings, the drill ground, the backwaters, the fort. The paths between them catch the light like threads. Nagoji small in the foreground, looking down at it.

> CAPTION: In Pune, the world had been simple. Orders came. Men rode. Villages paid. Priests blessed.

> CAPTION: Here, everything tied to everything else with threads I had not seen at first. A temple roof and a musket barrel and a pepper vine, all tugging on the same knot.

**6.3** The sea appearing beyond the inlet, a flat line of grey and blue. The two men at a turn in the path.

> RAMAYYAN: You see now why the Maharaja thinks of war even when he sits in a temple courtyard.

> NAGOJI: He is already at war. Even when the Dutch have not fired.

**6.4** Ramayyan, close, glancing up toward the fort above them. Three small insets strung along the bottom of the panel in a dark, desaturated tone: a bowman crouched on a palace roof; a hand tipping a vial into a lamp's oil; a dagger half drawn in a festival crowd. No victims shown.

> RAMAYYAN: He has been at war since before he took the throne. First with his own family. Then with the chieftains who thought him a boy.

> RAMAYYAN: The Lords of the Eight Houses. The temple yogakkar who thought the gods answered only to them. They sent assassins as often as petitions.

**6.5** Walking on, the fort's wall high above them now, dusk coming in off the sea.

> RAMAYYAN: Only after those knots were cut could he reach outward. Toward Kayamkulam. Then toward Dutch cannon.

> RAMAYYAN: Men like you feel war when the drums beat. Men like him feel it when the accounts do not balance.

## PAGE 7

Five panels. Borrowed eyes. Then the years turn, and the lessons are applied.

**7.1** Dusk at the coastal hall. Through the open doorway, men sit over palm leaves and cups of buttermilk, lamps just lit. Ramayyan has paused on the threshold and turned back.

> RAMAYYAN: You have a sharp eye, Sawant. You see angles on a battlefield. See these angles too.

> RAMAYYAN: When the time comes, the Maharaja will expect you to ride in ways that suit them. Not only your own.

**7.2** Nagoji, his hand resting on the hilt of his talwar.

> NAGOJI: He has my sword. My eyes he can borrow when he asks.

**7.3** Ramayyan regarding him for a long moment, as if tasting the answer. Half turned toward the lamplit hall.

> RAMAYYAN: Borrowed eyes.

> RAMAYYAN: We will see how long he keeps the loan.

> CAPTION: That was the first lesson. The application came soon after.

**7.4** Wide, one frame split into three vertical bands that flow into each other, left to right: riders fording a swollen river in driving rain; a dusty column climbing a northern pass under a hard sun; horses belly deep in monsoon mud, riders hauling at the bridles. Nagoji in each band.

> CAPTION: The months blurred into seasons, the seasons into years.

> CAPTION: Kayamkulam in the wet season. The northern passes in the dry. The monsoon, when even horses learned to pray for solid ground.

**7.5** A chieftain's gate. Padmini Amma (in her fifties, thick black hair coiled at the nape, simple gold, cotton sari hitched for walking, long walking stick) speaking to the chieftain on his own threshold. Nagoji stands silent by the gatepost. Behind him, filling the road, fifty mounted Maravar lancers, lances upright.

> CAPTION: A chieftain once pledged to one of the Eight Houses, and a grain levy.

> CAPTION: My presence, and the lancers behind me, turned a request into a requirement.

> CAPTION (separate, weighted): Other times, it was sharper.

## PAGE 8

Five panels. The raid near Attingal. Shoot it from outside and above; the point is the verdict in the courtyard, not the fighting.

**8.1** Wide. Dawn, blue mist. A madampi's estate: high old walls pressed round by thick groves of bamboo. On the wall, one watchman, yawning.

> CAPTION: A madampi near Attingal refused to send his men for the new army drills. He thought his high walls and thick bamboo would protect him from a young King's decrees.

> CAPTION: We took his walls at dawn.

**8.2** Low and fast, inside the bamboo: Maravar horsemen bursting through the wet stems at a gallop, Nagoji at their head, talwar drawn. Inset, tight: a palm leaf in Ramayyan's hand, the estate's supply paths scratched into it with a stylus.

> CAPTION: He did not know that Ramayyan had mapped his supply lines.

> CAPTION: Or that I had trained Maravar horsemen to cut through bamboo dampness as if it were dry grass.

**8.3** High and wide, distance instead of detail: the estate gate broken open, riders inside the walls, the defenders' spears thrown down in the dirt, smoke rising from a burning outbuilding. No bodies. No blood.

> CAPTION: There was no glory in it. Only the brutal efficiency of state-building.

**8.4** The smoking courtyard of an old noble house: carved wooden pillars, a tiled roof scorched at one corner, ash drifting. Marthanda Varma (V13 face; cream and gold, gold necklaces, bare-chested, spear grounded) standing. Before him the defeated chief, an older Nair lord in fine but disordered white, his sword belt unbuckled at his feet. Nagoji at the King's shoulder.

> CAPTION: A house that had been noble for three centuries.

> CAPTION: The King offered its chief a choice. Total submission, or total erasure.

**8.5** Riding back along a wet road, the column behind them. Varma beside Nagoji, both streaked with ash.

> CAPTION: The chief chose submission. His sons marched with us the next week.

> VARMA: You see? I do not destroy them because I hate them.

> VARMA: I break them so they can be reset into a stronger bone.

## PAGE 9

Five panels. He belongs now. The chapter closes on the whole kingdom at night, and something on the sea.

**9.1** An evening meal in the barracks. Nair soldiers and Maravar riders in a row on the floor, rice on plantain leaves. Nagoji among them, eating neatly with his right hand. The soldier beside him laughing at something Nagoji has just said.

> CAPTION: Somewhere in those years I stopped being a guest and became a limb of this growing beast.

> CAPTION: I learned enough Malayalam to order a charge or a retreat.

**9.2** Narrow strip, tight. A young Nair recruit at dusk ramming a charge down a musket barrel, the ramrod mid-stroke.

> CAPTION: And I learned that peace here was just a word for the time it took to reload.

**9.3** Night at Padmini Amma's estate. Nagoji alone, seated among the roots of a great jackfruit tree. Across the yard, lamplight and moving figures in the kalari; far off on its hill, the temple lit.

> CAPTION: By the time the Dutch began to show their teeth, I had ridden every road in the kingdom at least twice.

> CAPTION: And forgotten what the Deccan hills looked like, except in dreams.

**9.4** Close. Nagoji's left forearm resting on his knee, sleeve pushed back, the old Goa brand pale on the skin, the nails healed but ridged. Nothing on the wrist where the iron once was.

> CAPTION: In Goa, chains had held me still in one place.

> CAPTION: Here, invisible cords tied me to many. If one snapped, I had no doubt the others would tighten.

**9.5** Wide, full width, final. From the estate's rise at night: the dark kingdom laid out below, pinpricks of lamps at the hill temple, the fort, the market, the backwaters, joined by the faint pale lines of roads. Beyond them the sea, black. Far out on it, a single ship's lantern, too far to name.

> CAPTION: Travancore was already at war, even in its quiet moments. It had just learned to fight with more than swords.

> CAPTION (separate, weighted): If I wished to survive, I would have to learn the same.

## Adaptation notes

- **Where the pages went.** The chapter is a walking tour followed by a time jump, so most of it is exposition. The pages go to the scenes with a clash of voices: the temple (2 pages, because the "ledger" is the image the chapter's argument rests on), the market quarrel, the volley, Ibrahim, and the Attingal raid, which is the chapter's only action and gets a full page.
- **Opening compressed.** The novel's paragraph on reading a ruler through his forts, wells and cannon becomes one figure-less memory panel (1.1). It is figure-less because CONTINUITY.md has no look locked for Nagoji before his capture.
- **Temple.** Cut: Ayyappa as brother of Ganapati, and the aside about non-Brahmins being kept back (the staging of 1.5 carries it). The lamp-wall speech is trimmed but keeps its closing line verbatim.
- **Market.** The Arab merchant, the Syrian Christian scribe, chuckrams, candi and hundis are compressed into one establishing panel and two captions. The Yusuf and stallholder exchange is kept verbatim.
- **Flag for the author (3.5).** The novel reads "the queen in Padmanabhapuram cannot buy Dutch guns". The rest of the chapter speaks only of the king, so this looks like a slip. The script has "the palace" for now; restore the original if "the queen" is intended.
- **Drill master.** Left unnamed, as in the novel, and dressed in a stained brown coat so he cannot read as a British redcoat or a VOC blue-coat. The novel's "harsh, broken Hindustani" and his description are cut to the one caption "Not De Lannoy. Not yet."
- **Ramayyan's history of the King (6.4, 6.5).** The assassins become three wordless insets with no victims. Cut: "Some of those men died on pikes, some in exile, some under the weight of their own ruined walls." It is too long for the panel and too close to gore for the art rules.
- **Pune reflection trimmed.** Kept: "Orders came. Men rode. Villages paid. Priests blessed." Cut: "Merchants complained. Europeans were enemies to be raided..." The long list of what "tugged on the same knot" is cut to three items, and 6.2 draws the rest.
- **Years of consolidation.** The seasons become one three-band panel (7.4). The grain-levy visit with Padmini keeps its point in a caption. The King's offer to the madampi is given as a caption in the novel's words rather than as invented dialogue. His sons are carried by a caption, not drawn, because they march "the next week", not on the ride back. "Stronger bone" stays verbatim as the page's last line.
- **Ending.** The novel's closing itinerary ("Temple to market. Market to training ground...") is replaced by the night view in 9.5, which lays out the same chain as one image. The single ship's lantern on the horizon is an addition to hand off to Chapter 11, "Dutch on the Horizon"; cut it if the author wants no invention there. The brand in 9.4 follows CONTINUITY.md (left forearm); Chapter 9 calls the same mark a scar.
- **Verbatim lines kept.** "Walk with me, Sawant." / "Gods and I have an understanding..." / "A temple is not just stone. It is a ledger." / Yusuf and the stallholder / "Ram, my friend" / "Adequate." "Praise indeed." / "Water is a road..." / the horses, boats, temples and markets speech / "Men like you feel war when the drums beat..." / "Borrowed eyes. We will see how long he keeps the loan." / "reset into a stronger bone" / "peace here was just a word for the time it took to reload" / the last two lines.
- **Art.** Nagoji is on the commander sheet (chapters 7 to 16) throughout. The officiating Brahmin in 1.5 is the only figure who may use the V13 temple-priest sheet. The raid in 8.3 is shown at a distance with no bodies. There is no text in any frame; every caption and balloon is composited later.
